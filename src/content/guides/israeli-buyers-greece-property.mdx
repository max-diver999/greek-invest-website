**Quick answer:** Under the Israel–Greece double taxation treaty, income and gains from immovable
property are taxed **only** in the state where the property is situated. For an Israeli owner of Greek
property that means the Greek charge is the whole charge, and the Israeli return carries a disclosure
rather than a second bill. The planning question moves entirely onto the Greek side of the border.

**Greek requirements, in one place.** They do not vary by nationality and are not repeated here: [€400,000 and €800,000 tier map](/guides/greece-golden-visa-property-tiers-2026/) · [120 m² certified usable area test](/guides/greece-golden-visa-120-square-meter-rule/) · [short-term rental ban on the qualifying asset](/guides/greece-golden-visa-no-short-term-rental/) · [AFM and Greek bank account sequence](/guides/greece-golden-visa-bank-account-afm/) · [cadastre and engineer due diligence](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/). The treaty position below sits on top of all of them and changes none.

## Exclusive rights, not merely primary rights

Most tax treaties say the situs state **may** tax immovable property income, leaving the residence state
free to tax it too and give credit. The Israel–Greece treaty is drafted more strongly on this point:
Article 6 allocates income from immovable property to the state where the property sits, and Article
13(1) treats gains on its disposal the same way. The practical difference between may-tax and
allocated-to shows up on the disposal rather than on the rent. Greek capital gains tax on property is
currently suspended, so a sale during the suspension produces a Greek charge of nothing. Under a
conventional treaty the residence state would still tax the gain and simply have no foreign tax to credit
against it, which turns a Greek suspension into a transfer of the same money to another revenue
authority. Under this allocation there is no Israeli charge on that gain to begin with, so the suspension
is genuinely worth what it appears to be worth.

Put a number on the difference: on a €400,000 Crete deed sold for €480,000, the €80,000 gain attracts
nothing in Greece during the suspension and nothing in Israel under the allocation, while an American
owner of the same apartment carries the full €80,000 into a US return and pays there. Two owners, one
building, one sale price, and a difference measured in tens of thousands of euros that comes entirely
from which treaty each of them is standing under. The same logic runs through the holding period: on a
€400,000 apartment letting at €1,450 a month the Greek charge of roughly €2,932 is the whole tax on that
income, so an Israeli owner's five-year model needs one tax line where most nationalities need two and a
credit calculation between them. That is a
materially better position on an identical property, and it is the single most valuable line in the
treaty for an Israeli owner.

The practical difference is not academic. Under a credit treaty an owner pays the higher of the two
rates. Under exclusive allocation the owner pays the Greek rate and stops.

| | Credit-method treaty | Israel–Greece on immovable property |
|---|---|---|
| Greek rental income | Taxed in Greece, reported at home, credit given | Taxed in Greece only |
| Disposal gain | Taxed in Greece, reported at home, credit given | Taxed in Greece only |
| Net effective rate | The higher of the two | The Greek rate |
| Home-country return | Income reported, tax computed, credit claimed | Disclosure, no charge on this income |

Confirm the current treaty text and its application to your circumstances with an Israeli adviser before
relying on it. Treaty interpretation is fact-specific, and this page describes the general allocation
rather than your file.

## The tax disappears; the reporting does not

Exclusive taxing rights remove the Israeli charge on this income. They do not remove Israeli disclosure
obligations attaching to foreign assets and foreign accounts, and those obligations are independent of
whether any tax is due.

This is the single most common misunderstanding among Israeli buyers, and it has an expensive shape: an
owner concludes correctly that no Israeli tax arises, concludes incorrectly that nothing needs to be
filed, and discovers the distinction later. Establish with an Israeli accountant what has to be reported
and when, separately from the question of what is taxed.

## What that leaves you deciding: when to sell, not where to be taxed

Because Israel contributes no second charge, an Israeli owner's tax planning is Greek planning. That
concentrates the decision on one variable — the timing of a disposal against Greek rules.

Greek capital gains tax on property has been suspended, and a disposal during the suspension attracts no
Greek CGT. For an Israeli owner, with no home-country charge on the same gain, that means a sale inside
the window can be untaxed outright rather than merely deferred. That is a genuinely different position
from the American one on the equivalent page, where the same suspension moves the charge rather than
removing it.

Two cautions. The suspension has been extended more than once and the position at your disposal date is
what governs, so confirm it before building a plan around it. And selling the qualifying property ends
the basis for a Golden Visa unless another qualifying asset replaces it, which the
[selling after a Golden Visa guide](/guides/can-you-sell-property-after-golden-visa-greece/) sets out.

## Banking and source of funds is routine here

Greek compliance desks are familiar with Israeli buyers and the documentation that satisfies them is
standard: evidence of the funds in the sending account, the source of those funds, identification, and a
single bank-to-bank transfer in the buyer's own name as Circular 1/2026 requires.

The one item worth preparing early is certified translation. Hebrew-language bank records, employment
documents and sale contracts need translating for a Greek file, and doing that in advance shortens the
compliance step from weeks to days.

## A new or returning resident sits under a different set of rules entirely

This page has described the Israeli position for a settled Israeli tax resident. A significant share of
Israeli buyers are not that, and for them almost everything above is answered differently.

Israeli law gives new immigrants and returning residents a substantial holiday from Israeli tax on
foreign-source income and foreign assets, running for a defined period from the date residence begins.
The relief covers income arising abroad and gains on assets held abroad, and it has historically extended
to reporting as well as to tax, which is unusual — most exemptions of this kind remove the charge and
leave the filing.

For someone in that window, a Greek property produces a position that is genuinely unusual. The treaty
allocates the rental income and the disposal gain to Greece. Greece charges its rental tax and currently
suspends its capital gains tax on property. Israel, for the duration of the relief, is not charging the
person on foreign-source income at all. The result is a low-tax outcome arrived at legitimately from both
directions rather than through any structure.

The Golden Visa itself is unaffected by any of this. The relief is an Israeli tax status and the permit
is a Greek immigration one, and neither authority takes the other into account when deciding whether you
qualify.

Three cautions, and they matter more than the opportunity. The relief runs from a start date and it
ends — an asset bought early in the window can easily still be held after it closes, and the Israeli
position on that asset changes on that date without anything happening to the property. Second, the scope
of what is covered, and particularly what has to be reported, has been the subject of legislative
attention, so the rules that apply to someone starting the clock now are not necessarily the rules that
applied to somebody who started it years ago. Third, whether you qualify at all is a question of fact
about when Israeli residence began or resumed, and it is determined on evidence rather than intention.

Mortgage finance changes the shape again, because interest on a Greek loan sits against Greek income
under Greek rules rather than against the Israeli position this page describes, and the two do not
combine as an Israeli borrower would expect.

Currency deserves a line of its own here. The asset, the rent and the sale price are in euros while the
household's costs are in shekels, so the return measured at home moves with a rate nobody on either side
of the transaction controls.

The instruction that follows is specific. If you are inside a new or returning resident period, establish
with an Israeli accountant exactly when it ends before you buy, and plan the Greek holding period against
that date rather than against the Greek market alone. If you are not, this page's main analysis applies
and the exit timing discussion below is the relevant one. The two positions are different enough that
mixing advice between them is the most likely way for an Israeli buyer to get this wrong.

## Advantages and disadvantages for an Israeli buyer

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Greek tax is the entire tax on rental income and gains | The Greek rate applies in full, with no relief to soften it |
| A disposal in the suspension window can be untaxed on both sides | Disclosure obligations in Israel continue regardless |
| Compliance desks are familiar with Israeli source-of-funds files | Hebrew documentation needs certified translation |
| Planning is simplified to one jurisdiction | Selling the qualifying asset ends the permit basis |

## Risks an Israeli buyer should price before signing

- **Confusing no tax with no filing.** The two are separate questions with separate consequences.
- **Assuming the treaty covers the Golden Visa.** It governs taxation only; the 120 m² test, the
  single-deed rule and the letting ban are unaffected by any treaty.
- **Building an exit around the capital gains suspension** without confirming its current end date.
- **Selling the qualifying property** without a replacement asset or an alternative status in place.
- **Leaving translation to the compliance query** rather than doing it in advance.

## Buyer scenarios that fit the Israeli position

**The buyer wanting an EU foothold.** The permit is the product and the tax position is unusually clean.
Buy, hold, let long-term, and treat the Greek rate as the whole cost of the income.

**The buyer planning a medium-term exit.** This is where the Israeli position is genuinely advantageous
relative to other nationalities, and it is worth modelling the disposal date against the suspension
rather than against the market alone.

**The buyer intending to relocate.** Once you become a Greek tax resident the analysis changes shape
entirely and the Greek special regimes come into play; the
[Article 5A non-dom guide](/guides/greece-non-dom-tax-regime-5a-guide/) is the starting point for that
conversation with advisers on both sides.

## Sizing the only tax bill there is

Because Greece takes the whole charge, there is one calculation rather than two. On a €400,000 apartment
letting at €1,450 a month, gross rent is €17,400, the Greek bill is roughly €2,932, and the effective rate
on gross is 16.9%. ENFIA follows, then management, vacancy and maintenance. Nothing further is added at
home, which is why an Israeli buyer can model a Greek asset with a confidence that most nationalities on
this site cannot. The number to carry into a spreadsheet is that effective rate on gross — 16.9% on this
property — rather than a marginal band, and the number not to carry is any blended international figure,
because for this nationality there is no second jurisdiction to blend with.

It also means the usual
sequencing advice reverses. Buyers of most nationalities are told to establish the home-country treatment
first, because that is where the surprises live. An Israeli buyer's surprises are all on the Greek side,
in the tier that applies to the parcel and the certified area on the deed, and the tax question is
settled before it is asked.

## The reporting obligations that survive the treaty

Israeli disclosure requirements attach to foreign assets and foreign accounts independently of whether tax
is payable on them. The treaty allocates the taxing right; it says nothing about filing.

That distinction produces a specific and avoidable failure. An owner establishes correctly that no Israeli
tax arises on the Greek rent, concludes that the Israeli return has nothing to say about the property, and
files accordingly. The obligation was never about the tax.

Establish three things with an Israeli accountant before the first Greek tax year closes: what has to be
reported and on which form, whether the Greek bank account itself is separately reportable, and what the
position is on the eventual disposal even where no Israeli charge arises. All three are cheap questions
asked early and expensive ones asked late.

## Timing the exit, which is the decision this page exists to inform

For most nationalities on this site the sale decision is a two-country calculation. For an Israeli owner
it is a one-country calculation, which makes the timing question unusually clean and unusually
consequential.

| Consideration | Where it sits |
|---|---|
| Greek capital gains charge | Suspended at present; confirm the position at your disposal date |
| Israeli charge on the same gain | None, under the treaty's allocation of immovable property gains |
| Reporting on disposal | Israeli disclosure obligations continue regardless |
| Effect on the residence permit | Selling the qualifying asset ends the permit basis unless replaced |

The last row is the constraint that binds in practice. A permit holder who sells to capture an untaxed
gain has to either replace the qualifying asset, have already naturalised, or accept losing the status the
purchase was made for. That is a sequencing problem rather than a tax problem, and it is worth modelling
at purchase rather than at sale.

One further point of sequencing. If you expect to become a Greek tax resident at some stage, the treaty analysis on this page describes your position as an Israeli resident holding Greek property, not as a Greek resident holding it. The allocation of immovable property income does not change, but everything else about your position does, and the order in which you buy and move is worth settling with advisers on both sides first.
