**Quick answer:** Athens prices range from about €5,409 per square metre in Kolonaki
and Lykavittos to about €1,731 in Patisia, with the southern Riviera suburbs in a
separate band above the central average. The whole of Attica falls in the €800,000
Golden Visa tier regardless of local prices. Athens yields about 5.43% gross against
a 4.40% national average, and it is the only Greek market where rent is supported by
year-round demand rather than by a season. It is also now the slowest-growing segment
in the country.

## The threefold spread inside one city

| Where | Asking price per m² | Source and reference |
|---|---:|---|
| Kolonaki and Lykavittos | about €5,409 | Indomio, June 2026 |
| Voula and Varkiza, villa stock | roughly €5,000 to €6,500 | Market summaries, 2026 |
| Glyfada | roughly €4,250 to €5,000 | Market summaries, 2026 |
| Patisia | about €1,731 | Indomio, June 2026 |

The two Indomio figures are asking prices from live listings for central Athens
municipalities. The Riviera bands come from published market summaries rather than a
single portal index, which is why they are given as ranges.

A factor of three inside one metropolitan area is unusual and it is the most important
thing to understand about buying here. In most European capitals the gap between a
prime central district and an ordinary residential one is large but not this large.
In Athens the same budget buys a small flat in Kolonaki or a substantial one in
Patisia, and the two are a twenty-minute journey apart.

## Athens is the only Greek market that works in February

Every island page on this site ends up making the same seasonal point. Athens is the
page where it does not apply, and that is the reason to consider it.

Rent here is supported by employment, universities, hospitals, government and a
permanent population. Demand does not stop in October. The practical consequences run
through every part of a purchase: a property can be let on a twelve-month basis
without the income collapsing, works can be commissioned in January, tradespeople are
available, and a resale can be viewed in any month of the year.

That is why Athens carries an average gross rental yield around 5.43% against a
national average of 4.40%, despite being among the more expensive places to buy. The
[market overview](/property-for-sale/) sets the yield picture out in full. Islands
beat Athens on peak nightly rates and lose to it across twelve months, which is the
whole difference between a seasonal asset and a year-round one.

## Which part of Athens, by what you want

| If you want | Look at | Trade |
|---|---|---|
| Coast and city together | Glyfada, Voula, Vouliagmeni, Varkiza | Highest prices per m², car-dependent |
| Walkability and letting demand | Kolonaki, Koukaki, Pangrati, Mets | Small units, older stock, tighter short-let rules |
| Family space and schools | Kifissia, Marousi, Psychiko and the northern suburbs | Commute, less coastal |
| Yield on paper | Western and northwestern districts | Thin foreign resale pool |

The Riviera is what most international buyers picture when they picture Athens
property, and it behaves like a separate market: coastal, lower density, newer stock,
and priced accordingly. It is also the part of the city that has absorbed the most
development attention over recent years.

The central districts are where letting demand is deepest and where stock is oldest.
Athenian apartment buildings from the 1960s and 1970s dominate the centre, which
means the [engineer's assessment](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/) does real
work here on wiring, plumbing, insulation and seismic retrofitting rather than on
unpermitted extensions.

## Buying into a Greek apartment block

Almost every Athens purchase is a flat in a polykatoikia, the mid-century apartment
building that defines the city, and it works differently from a house.

You buy a specific apartment plus an undivided share of the building and the plot,
expressed as a percentage on the deed. That share sets your vote in building
decisions and your contribution to shared costs, and it is a number worth reading
rather than skimming.

Shared costs, the koinochrista, cover the lift, stairwell lighting, cleaning, any
central heating and the building's maintenance. On a block with central oil heating
they can be substantial in winter, and on a block that has deferred maintenance for
years they can arrive as a large one-off levy after a vote you were not present for.

Three things to establish before an offer, none of which appears in a listing.
Whether the building has an active management and a maintenance record. What the
current monthly shared charge actually is, not what the seller says it usually is.
And whether any major works have been voted on or are being discussed, because a
lift replacement or a facade repair divides across the shares and yours arrives with
the keys.

This is the Athens equivalent of the island legality question: a normal, manageable
feature of the market that costs money when discovered late.

## Transport is priced into everything

The metro is the variable that most reliably separates two otherwise similar Athens
addresses, and it is not always obvious from a map.

A property within a short walk of a metro station lets faster, lets to a wider pool
and resells to a wider pool, because a large share of Athens renters do not have a car
and the traffic makes one a mixed blessing. The tram along the coast performs the same
function for the Riviera at a slower pace, and the suburban rail serves the northern
suburbs and the airport.

What this means practically is that walking distance to a station is worth paying for
in a way that a slightly better view is not, if the purchase has any letting or resale
intention at all. It is also the reason some cheaper districts yield well on paper:
they are cheap partly because they are awkward to reach, and the same awkwardness is
waiting when you sell.

## The Golden Visa problem specific to Athens

All of Attica sits in the €800,000 tier under Law 5100/2024. That is straightforward
in Kolonaki, where €800,000 buys roughly 148 square metres at prevailing asking
prices, and it is awkward everywhere else.

At Patisia prices near €1,731 per square metre, €800,000 corresponds to roughly 462
square metres, which is not a normal apartment. The tier does not follow the local
price level, so in the cheaper half of the city a qualifying purchase means buying
something considerably larger or more expensive than the area's own market suggests.

Two consequences follow for anyone combining a purchase with a residence application.
Buyers at the €800,000 threshold concentrate in the districts where that sum buys a
normal property, which is a narrower set than the city as a whole. And the
[€800,000 areas guide](/guides/athens-golden-visa-800000-areas/) is worth reading
before shortlisting, because the tier is set by the cadastral parcel rather than the
address and the boundary questions in Attica are real.

The [tier structure](/guides/greece-golden-visa-property-tiers-2026/) sets out how the
zones are defined and where the €400,000 alternative applies elsewhere in Greece.

## Letting, and two rules that can both apply

Athens is where the short-let question is most contested and most regulated.

Restrictions on new short-term rental registrations in parts of the centre have
tightened, and the position is now location-specific enough that it belongs in the
pre-offer checks rather than in the post-purchase planning. The
[Athens moratorium guide](/guides/athens-short-term-rental-moratorium-2026/) covers
where the restrictions apply.

Separately, a Golden Visa qualifying property may only be let on leases of twelve
months or longer anywhere in Greece. A central Athens Golden Visa purchase can
therefore be caught by both rules simultaneously: prohibited from short-letting by
the permit, and in a district where new registrations are restricted anyway.

For an ordinary purchase the long-let market in Athens is genuinely strong, which is
the point of buying here rather than on an island. The
[rental income guide](/guides/greece-rental-income-tax-non-resident/) covers the tax
position, which reaches 45% from 2026 with only a flat 5% deduction.

## Athens is now the slowest-growing part of Greece

On Bank of Greece data for the first quarter of 2026, Athens apartment prices rose
5.2% year on year, against 6.4% in Thessaloniki, 5.4% in other cities and 6.9% in
areas outside all of those.

For most of the previous cycle Athens led, absorbing the attention and the capital
while the regions lagged. That has reversed, and a great deal of commentary about the
Greek market has not caught up with it.

Two readings are both reasonable. Athens may simply be further through its cycle,
having repriced earlier. Or the regions may be moving further on fewer transactions,
which thin markets do. The data does not choose, and confident assertions either way are
opinion rather than finding.

## Risks and red flags

**Old central stock.** Wiring, plumbing, insulation and seismic standards on 1960s and
1970s apartment blocks. The survey matters more here than the title search.

**Assuming the Golden Visa threshold matches the district.** It does not. €800,000
applies across Attica whatever the local price level.

**Short-let assumptions in restricted areas.** Check the registration position for the
specific address before an offer, not after.

**Buying on Riviera photographs without the commute.** The southern suburbs are a car
journey from the centre, and the coastal road is the constraint people underestimate.

**Reading Athens as the growth story.** It is currently the slowest of the four
segments, which does not make it a bad purchase and does make the standard narrative
out of date.

**Skipping the building before buying the flat.** In a city of apartment blocks the
condition and governance of the block matter as much as the unit inside it, and a
well-presented flat in a badly run building is a worse purchase than the reverse.

## Buyer scenarios

**A buyer with €300,000 wanting a lettable central apartment.** Achievable in several
central districts, with the strongest case being walkable areas where long-let demand
is deep. The survey is the priority, not the title. Yields around the Athens average
of 5.43% gross are realistic; net is materially lower after the 2026 rental scale.

**A buyer with €800,000 taking a Golden Visa file.** Kolonaki at about €5,409 per
square metre gives roughly 148 square metres, which is a normal large apartment. The
same sum in a cheaper district buys something that is not a normal property for that
area, which is worth thinking about at resale.

**A buyer wanting a coastal apartment on the Riviera.** Glyfada at roughly €4,250 to
€5,000 per square metre, or Voula and Varkiza higher for villa stock. The trade is a
car-dependent life and a coastal road that is the real commute, against the only part
of Attica where you get sea and city together. Tram proximity does for the Riviera
what metro proximity does for the centre.

**A family relocating with school-age children.** The northern suburbs, where
international schooling and space are concentrated, at the cost of a commute and no
coast. Renting for a year before buying is the standard advice and it is correct
here, because Athens neighbourhoods differ more than their prices suggest.
