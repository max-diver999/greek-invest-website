**Quick answer:** Piraeus is Attica's port-city investment corridor, Greece's largest commercial harbour, a cruise hub, and a gentrifying residential municipality west of central Athens. Suburban asking prices often start around €2,400 per square metre; Kastella and Mikrolimano run higher. The entire municipality sits in the €800,000 Golden Visa zone with the 120m² single-property rule. Gross long-term yields typically land 5.0 to 6.5%, between Athens centre at 5.43% and working-class northern districts at 6.0 to 7.5%. Regeneration signals such as Piraeus Gate (1,000+ planned homes) support a long hold thesis, but port logistics, building vintage, and title quality still determine whether a specific unit works.

**Quick answer:** Piraeus is Attica, so a Golden Visa file needs €800,000 on one deed with 120 square
metres of certified main usable area. What distinguishes it from every other Attica address is that the
port is a participant in the property market rather than a backdrop to it: freight routing, terminal
operations and berth traffic set which streets are lettable and which carry a permanent discount, and
they do it at a granularity of a few hundred metres.

It is also a city in its own right — its own municipality, its own commercial centre, its own working
population — connected to Athens by Metro Line 1 and the tram. That makes it a competitor for tenants
rather than a dormitory.

## The harbour is an employer and a boundary at the same time

Piraeus is the largest passenger port in Europe and a major container gateway, and both halves of that
matter to a residential buyer in opposite directions.

As an employer it produces exactly the tenant a Golden Visa asset needs. Shipping companies, freight
forwarding, marine services, customs, the ferry operators and the businesses attached to them staff the
city year-round, and a twelve-month residential lease — the only letting a qualifying deed may carry —
has a straightforward counterparty here.

As an operation it draws hard lines through the map. Container terminals run continuously. Freight
concentrates on specific roads and does not move. Cruise and ferry berths generate seasonal pedestrian
surges on particular streets. A flat that faces one of these is discounted permanently, and a flat two
blocks inland is not.

Three checks belong on foot rather than on a map, and all three are cheap:

- **What the windows face at seven on a weekday morning**, because port operations are scheduled, not
  ambient.
- **Which road the trucks use**, since freight routing is stable and visible.
- **The walk to the Metro or tram**, which is what a professional tenant is buying and what protects the
  rent when a shipping cycle turns.

## Kastella, the centre and the grid are three different markets

Piraeus is not one price. The hillside above the harbour, the commercial centre and the flat working grid
behave differently enough that comparing them on a single €/m² figure misleads.

| Sub-market | What it is | What it means for a long let |
|---|---|---|
| Kastella and the hill | Sea aspect, quieter, lifestyle stock | Premium paid per square metre of view, which the 120 m² certificate does not count |
| Central Piraeus | The commercial and administrative core | Deepest year-round demand, closest to transport |
| The port grid | Flat working district near the terminals | Lowest entry, most exposed to freight and operations |
| Marina and Zea | Yacht basin frontage, restaurants | Priced on amenity, thinner tenant pool |

For a Golden Visa buyer the useful question is not which is best but which the €800,000 lands in. On the
hill the view premium pushes the price per certified square metre up, so the size test tightens. In the
grid the threshold sits well above ordinary local transaction levels, so the buyer is entering a thin
boutique layer rather than the local market. The

## A city, not a commute

Piraeus has a municipal government, a university, a court complex, hospitals and a commercial centre that
serves its own population rather than overflow from Athens. Metro Line 1 runs to Monastiraki and Omonia;
the tram runs along the coast; the suburban rail connects to the airport corridor.

The practical consequence for an owner is that vacancy here is not driven by Athens. A tenant working in
Piraeus does not have an Athens alternative that is equally convenient, which is a materially different
position from a suburb where every tenant is choosing between locations. It also means Piraeus rents move
on shipping and local employment, and those are not the same cycle as central Athens offices.

## What the €800,000 threshold does in a city that transacts below it

Ordinary Piraeus residential stock sits far below the Golden Visa floor. That is not a problem to be
solved but a structure to be understood: the qualifying purchase is not a bigger version of the local
transaction, it is a different product bought by a different buyer.

Two consequences follow. Comparables are thin, because there are few genuine €800,000 residential
transactions in the municipality outside new boutique schemes, so a valuation here rests on the scheme
rather than on the street. And the rent does not scale with the price — a unit that cost four times the
local average does not let for four times the local rent, so the yield compresses. Neither is a reason to
avoid Piraeus; both are reasons to buy it for the permit and the tenant rather than for the income
multiple.

## Older stock and what an engineer can certify

Much of the city's residential fabric is mid-century apartment building, and the usual Greek questions
apply with force: enclosed verandas, converted basements, rooftop additions, and internal changes that
never reached a permit. On a Golden Visa file those are not negotiating points but eligibility items,
because an un-legalised alteration does not count toward main usable area.

Ask for the Electronic Building Identity and an engineer's certificate stating enclosed area before the
preliminary agreement. On port-city stock the gap between the marketed figure and the certified one is
frequently wide, and the [due diligence guide](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/) covers the rest of
the file.

| Lowest entry per square metre | The port grid | Check freight routing and what the windows face before anything else |
| Yield-focused | Inner Athens instead | Piraeus rents do not scale with a Golden Visa purchase price |

### Risks and red flags specific to this city

Port exposure is the one that does not improve: terminal operations, freight roads and berth traffic are
fixed infrastructure, and a discounted street stays discounted. Mid-century apartment stock carries the
usual unpermitted alterations, which do not count toward main usable area. And a shipping-cycle downturn
affects local employment directly, so a tenant base that looks deep in a good year is more cyclical than
an Athens office district.

## Piraeus in one paragraph

Piraeus is Athens' port city and its most-improved investment story of the past decade: metro line 3 now reaches the port, the cruise and ferry terminals anchor year-round employment, and Kastella and Mikrolimano carry genuine sea-view scarcity. It falls inside the €800,000 Attica prime tier, so the Golden Visa arithmetic is demanding, but long-term gross yields on renovated stock run higher than the southern Riviera because entry prices are lower against comparable rents. The risks are specific rather than general: older building stock with legalisation history, uneven block-by-block quality, and a port-adjacent noise and traffic profile that varies sharply within a few hundred metres.
