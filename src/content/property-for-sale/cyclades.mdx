**Quick answer:** Cycladic prices are the highest in Greece and vary by island more
than by anything else. Mykonos asks about €7,623 per square metre, Oia on Santorini
about €5,742 and Thira about €4,363, with Paros and Naxos materially below. Mykonos
and Santorini sit in the €800,000 Golden Visa tier while smaller Cycladic islands sit
in the €400,000 one. The season is short, which is why nightly rates are high and why
annual yields are not, and a Golden Visa property cannot use the short-let model at
all.

## What the islands ask

| Island or area | Asking price per m² | Source and reference |
|---|---:|---|
| Mykonos | about €7,623 | Indomio, September 2025 |
| Santorini, Oia | about €5,742 | Indomio, July 2026 |
| Santorini, Thira | about €4,363 | Indomio, July 2026 |
| Paros | roughly €4,950 to €5,454 | Market summaries, 2026 |
| Naxos | roughly €3,700 to €4,800 | Market summaries, 2025 |

The Mykonos and Santorini figures come from Indomio, a Greek property portal, and are
asking prices from live listings rather than recorded transactions. The Paros and
Naxos ranges come from published market summaries rather than a single portal index,
which is why they are given as bands.

Two comparisons put the numbers in proportion. Against the Ionian, Mykonos is roughly
three times [Kefalonia](/property-for-sale/kefalonia/) at a little under €2,400 and
about two and a half times [Zakynthos](/property-for-sale/zakynthos/) at €2,889.
Against the mainland it is more than four times the Peloponnese at €1,778.

## The premium is for recognition, not for land

Naxos is larger than Mykonos, greener, has more water, better agriculture and
arguably better beaches, and costs a fraction of it. That single comparison explains
the Cycladic price structure better than any analysis of amenities.

What the top of this market prices is international recognition and the airlift that
follows it. Mykonos and Santorini have direct international flights through the
season, a global brand and a buyer pool that includes people who are not comparing
against other Greek islands at all. Naxos and the smaller Cyclades have Greek
domestic connections, ferry access and a buyer pool that is mostly European.

For a buyer this produces a clean decision rather than a difficult one. If the point
is the name, the price is what the name costs. If the point is Cycladic landscape,
white villages, dry stone and blue water, the same landscape is available at half the
price two islands away.

## The season is the whole trade

Cycladic nightly rates are the highest in Greece. Cycladic seasons are among the
shortest.

Ferry frequency, flight schedules and the businesses that depend on them concentrate
into roughly May to early October, with the genuinely strong weeks much narrower than
that. On the smaller islands a substantial share of restaurants, shops and services
close entirely for winter, and on the largest ones the town stays open while the
resort areas do not.

That is the trade, and it is worth stating in both directions. Strong income
concentrated into a short window is genuinely strong: a well-placed Cycladic property
can earn in ten weeks what a mainland flat earns in a year. It is also concentrated
risk, since a poor season has nowhere to recover, and it means the property is idle
and still costing money for seven or eight months.

Anyone modelling a Cycladic purchase on annualised occupancy is modelling the wrong
island. The number that matters is peak-season rate multiplied by realistic peak-season
weeks, minus a full year of holding costs.

## The islands most buyers never consider

The Cyclades are twenty-odd inhabited islands and the search traffic goes to four of
them. The rest are where the value is, and they are genuinely different places rather
than cheaper versions of the same one.

**Syros** is the administrative capital of the Cyclades and the one island in the
group with a real year-round city. Ermoupoli is a neoclassical port town with a
hospital, courts, a university department and a population that does not leave in
October. It is the answer for anyone who wants Cycladic geography without accepting
Cycladic seasonality, and it is priced well below the famous islands because holiday
buyers do not look at it.

**Tinos** is fifteen minutes from Mykonos by ferry and a different economy entirely,
with a marble-carving tradition, hundreds of dovecotes and villages that stay
inhabited. It has become the quiet alternative for buyers who have priced Mykonos and
decided against it.

**Milos** trades on volcanic geology and a coastline unlike anywhere else in Greece,
with a shorter international profile and correspondingly lower prices than its beaches
would suggest.

**Sifnos, Serifos, Folegandros and Amorgos** are smaller again, with proportionally
smaller markets, thinner services and the sharpest winters. Buying on any of them is a
lifestyle decision rather than a market one, and resale depends on finding another
person who wanted that specific island.

The pattern worth noticing is that price tracks how easy an island is to reach from
outside Greece, not how good it is to be on. Anyone willing to change at Athens or
take a longer ferry is buying at a discount for that inconvenience.

## Getting there, and what it does to value

Access is the variable that sets Cycladic prices, so it is worth being explicit about
what the differences are.

Mykonos and Santorini have direct international flights through the season. That
brings a buyer pool that is not comparing against other Greek islands, and it brings
guests who book without a connecting journey. It is the single largest reason those
two carry the prices they do.

Paros and Naxos have domestic airports with Athens connections plus fast ferries.
Good enough for a European buyer, one step removed for anyone flying long-haul.

Syros, Tinos, Milos and the smaller islands are ferry-first. Some have small airports
with limited domestic service. The journey from a long-haul arrival is a day rather
than an afternoon.

For an owner this shows up in three places: how often you actually use the property,
how easy it is to let and to whom, and how quickly it sells when you want out. The
last one is the one people discover late. A thin buyer pool is fine while you are
buying and it is the whole problem when you are selling.

## Golden Visa: the tier splits the archipelago

| Island | Tier | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mykonos | €800,000 | Named in the prime zone under Law 5100/2024 |
| Santorini | €800,000 | Named in the prime zone |
| Larger Cyclades above the population threshold | €800,000 | Population threshold |
| Smaller Cycladic islands | €400,000 | Below the threshold |

The practical effect is that the two islands most buyers want are the two that cost
twice as much to qualify with. The
[tier guide](/guides/greece-golden-visa-property-tiers-2026/) sets out the zones, and
the tier follows the cadastral parcel rather than the island's reputation, so it
should be confirmed against the land registry before an offer.

The restriction that follows matters more here than anywhere. A qualifying property
may only be let on leases of twelve months or longer, and short-term letting is
prohibited for the life of the permit. On an island where the entire income case is
the July and August nightly rate, that removes the income case rather than trimming
it. The
[short-term letting rule](/guides/greece-golden-visa-no-short-term-rental/) sets out
what counts and what renewal checks.

## What the premium actually costs to hold

Purchase price is the visible number and the annual cost is the one that decides
whether people keep these properties.

Running costs scale with value and with exposure. ENFIA is assessed on the objective
value, which on a Mykonos or Santorini property is high. Insurance on a coastal
building in a high-wind, high-salt environment is not the same product as insurance
on a mainland flat. Pools, cisterns and desalination equipment need servicing whether
or not anyone visits. And on the smaller islands, getting a tradesperson to a property
in February may mean paying for their journey as well as their time.

Add a management arrangement, which almost every absentee owner ends up needing, and
the annual figure on a Cycladic house is materially higher than the same floor area on
the mainland. That is not an argument against buying. It is an argument for putting
the holding cost into the decision at the start, because it is the number that turns a
good purchase into a reluctant sale three years later.

The version of this that catches people is the property bought at the top of a budget
with nothing left over. A Cycladic house is an expensive thing to own quietly through
a soft year, and the buyers who enjoy them are generally the ones who bought below
their ceiling rather than at it.

## What to check in the Cyclades specifically

The general [due diligence sequence](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/) applies.
Four things carry extra weight on these islands.

**Traditional settlement rules.** Many Cycladic villages are protected settlements
with strict controls on external appearance, materials, colour, window proportions
and roof form. They are the reason the islands look the way they do and they
substantially constrain what you can alter.

**Water.** Several islands rely on desalination and cistern storage. A property's
water arrangement is a real question rather than a formality, particularly for
anything with a pool.

**Building legality.** The [engineer's certificate](/guides/greece-property-engineer-certificate/)
carries the usual weight, and older village stock with terraces enclosed over the
decades is common.

**Access.** Not every attractive plot has a made-up road, and on stepped village
sites vehicle access may end some distance from the door. That affects building
costs, resale and how the property is actually used.

## Buyer scenarios

**A buyer with €400,000 wanting a Cycladic house.** Naxos, Paros or a smaller island,
where that budget corresponds to roughly 80 to 110 square metres depending on
location. Not Mykonos, where it corresponds to about 52 square metres, and not a
Golden Visa file on Mykonos or Santorini, where the threshold is €800,000.

**A buyer with €800,000 taking a Golden Visa file on Santorini.** Qualifies at Thira
prices near €4,363 with room, or in Oia near €5,742 more tightly. The property must be
a single dwelling on one deed with at least 120 square metres of main usable area, and
the letting restriction removes the short-let income entirely. Whether the purchase
still makes sense depends on wanting the house rather than the yield.

**A buyer modelling rental income.** The Cyclades are the wrong end of Greece for
yield. Purchase prices are the highest in the country and the earning window is among
the shortest, and the Greek rental income scale reaching 45% from 2026 compresses the
net further. The [market overview](/property-for-sale/) sets out where yields
actually sit.

## Risks and red flags

**Modelling annual occupancy on a seasonal island.** The single most common error in
Cycladic investment cases.

**Assuming the tier from the island name.** Mykonos and Santorini are prime zone;
neighbouring islands may not be, and the parcel decides.

**Buying inside a protected settlement without checking what you may change.** The
constraint is real and it is not negotiable.

**Underestimating the water and access questions**, both of which cost money to solve
after purchase and nothing to check before it.

**Reading luxury segment figures as the market.** Reported median offer prices in the
prime resort segment run far above the general asking averages on the same islands,
and the two are frequently quoted interchangeably.
