**Quick answer:** Everything about buying Greek property is the same for an American as for anyone else.
Everything about *owning* it is different, because the United States taxes its citizens and green card
holders on worldwide income wherever they live, and every other country in this series taxes on
residence. The consequences run in both directions: a Greek tax break can cost an American money, and
moving to Greece does not switch the US obligation off.

**Not on this page.** The Greek half of the transaction is the same for Americans as for anyone, and it lives elsewhere on this site: the [€400,000 and €800,000 tier map](/guides/greece-golden-visa-property-tiers-2026/), the [120 m² certified usable area test](/guides/greece-golden-visa-120-square-meter-rule/), the [short-term rental ban on the qualifying asset](/guides/greece-golden-visa-no-short-term-rental/), the [AFM and Greek bank account sequence](/guides/greece-golden-visa-bank-account-afm/) and the [cadastre and engineer due diligence](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/). What follows is only the part that turns on your passport.

## Citizenship-based taxation, and why it changes the arithmetic

The United States taxes citizens and green card holders on worldwide income regardless of residence.
Greece, like every other country covered in this series, taxes on residence. A Greek purchase therefore
creates US filing consequences whether or not you ever spend a night in the property, and relocating to
Athens does not end them.

That asymmetry is the reason a page like this exists at all. For a Canadian, an Australian or a German,
the interesting question is what happens if they move. For an American, nothing about moving changes the
obligation, so the interesting question is how the two systems interact while both are running.

## The 1950 treaty works on credit, not exemption

The US–Greece income tax convention signed in 1950 is one of the oldest bilateral treaties still in
force, and it works on the **credit** method rather than the exemption method.

In practice that means Greek rental income is taxed first in Greece and then reported again on the US
return, with the Greek tax claimed back as a foreign tax credit on Form 1116. Where the Greek rate is at
or above the US rate on the same income, the credit generally absorbs the US liability and the American
pays the higher of the two. Where the Greek rate is lower, the difference is paid to the IRS.

| Greek rental income band | Greek rate | Effect for a US filer |
|---|---|---|
| Up to €12,000 | 15% | Credit is small; the balance is likely payable in the US |
| €12,001 to €24,000 | 25% | Credit larger; top-up depends on your US bracket |
| €24,001 to €35,000 | 35% | Credit often absorbs the US liability |
| Above €35,000 | 45% | Excess credit generally cannot be refunded, only carried |

Note the base as well as the rate. Greece taxes rental income on **gross rent**, with only a flat 5%
repairs allowance set against it, while the US taxes net rental profit after actual expenses and
depreciation. The two systems are computing different numbers, and it is common for a property to show a
US loss and a Greek tax bill in the same year.

## The suspension that costs you money

Greek capital gains tax on property has been suspended, so a disposal during the suspension attracts no
Greek CGT. For a German, a Briton or an Australian who is not Greek-resident, that is straightforwardly
good news.

For an American it is the opposite of good news. The entire gain remains taxable in the United States,
and because no Greek tax was paid there is no foreign tax credit to set against it. The suspension does
not reduce the tax on the gain; it moves the whole of it from Athens to Washington.

This is the single most counter-intuitive item on the page, and it is worth stating in its strongest
form: **the one nationality for whom the Greek capital gains suspension is not a benefit is the one most
likely to be told it is.** Confirm the suspension's current end date with your lawyer before modelling a
disposal, because it has been extended more than once and the position at your sale date is what governs.

## FBAR and FATCA, triggered by a rule you cannot opt out of

Circular 1/2026 requires the purchase price to arrive from abroad through a Greek credit institution into
an account in the buyer's own name. A Greek bank account is therefore not optional on a Golden Visa
purchase — and for Americans that mandatory account is itself a reporting event.

| Obligation | Trigger | Where it is filed |
|---|---|---|
| FinCEN Form 114 (FBAR) | Aggregate foreign accounts exceed USD 10,000 at any point in the year | Separately from the tax return, to FinCEN |
| Form 8938 (FATCA) | Higher thresholds, varying by filing status and residence | With the Form 1040 |
| Greek bank reporting | Automatic, under FATCA intergovernmental arrangements | By the bank, to the IRS |

Two points that catch people. The FBAR threshold is an aggregate across all foreign accounts and it is
measured at the highest balance at any moment, not at year end — a purchase completing through the
account will cross it. And the real property itself is not an FBAR item; the account that buys it is.

## What is genuinely different about the EB-5 comparison

Americans are the only readers of this series who have a domestic residence-by-investment programme to
compare against, and the comparison is not like for like.

EB-5 is an immigrant visa leading to US permanent residence, priced at a materially higher capital
commitment than the Greek thresholds, carrying a job-creation requirement, an at-risk investment
structure and a processing queue that varies substantially by country of chargeability. The Greek Golden
Visa is a residence permit in another country, bought through a deed you own outright, with no job
creation and no at-risk requirement.

They answer different questions. EB-5 is for somebody who wants to live in the United States. The Greek
permit is for an American who wants a foothold in the EU without giving up anything at home. Anyone
weighing them on capital alone is comparing a residence-by-investment programme with a property purchase
that happens to carry a permit — and should check the current USCIS minimums directly, because they were
raised and indexed after 2022 and older comparisons circulating online are out of date.

## What the permit actually solves for an American

US passport holders enter the Schengen area visa-free but are bound by the 90 days in any rolling
180-day period rule. That is the constraint the Greek permit removes: a residence permit holder is not
counting days in Greece, and the permit carries no minimum stay requirement.

It does not make you an EU citizen, it does not give you the right to work across the EU, and it does not
change your US tax position by a single line. It buys presence.

## Holding it through a company is where American files go wrong

The structuring instinct that serves an American well domestically is actively dangerous here. A US
person who takes title to Greek property through a non-US company — a Greek entity, an offshore holding
vehicle, anything a European adviser might suggest for succession or privacy — has created a foreign
corporation in the US tax system, and the US tax system has strong opinions about those.

Depending on the facts, the entity can fall into the controlled foreign corporation rules or the passive
foreign investment company rules. Both are regimes designed to stop Americans deferring tax by parking
income offshore, and both are punitive rather than neutral: additional annual information returns with
substantial penalties for late or missing filings, income attributed to the shareholder whether or not
anything is distributed, and in the PFIC case a charge that can exceed what direct ownership would have
cost. None of this depends on the arrangement being aggressive. It depends only on there being a foreign
company with a US shareholder.

State-level treatment is the other half nobody checks. A state does not necessarily follow the federal
treaty position, so a resident of a state that taxes worldwide income can face a charge the federal
credit does not reach.

The practical instruction is short. Do not accept a holding structure recommended by a Greek or other
European adviser without a US adviser confirming its US treatment first, and be aware that direct
personal ownership of the Greek property — the simplest option, and the one that satisfies the Golden
Visa's single-deed requirement most cleanly anyway — is usually also the cleanest US position.

A joint purchase with a non-US spouse needs the same check before completion rather than after, because
who holds title decides whose return the property lands on and the answer is not always the one the
couple assumed.

The same instinct catches Americans on the banking side. FATCA obliges foreign financial institutions to
identify and report US account holders, and the compliance cost of doing that has made some non-US banks
reluctant to onboard Americans at all. Greek banks do open accounts for US persons, and Circular 1/2026
requires you to have one, but expect the identification paperwork to be heavier than a European buyer's
and expect the process to take longer. That is a scheduling fact rather than an obstacle, and the way to
manage it is to start the account before you need it rather than when the deposit is due.

## Advantages and disadvantages for a US buyer specifically

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Removes the Schengen 90/180 constraint with no minimum stay | Every Greek tax event is also a US tax event, permanently |
| The 1950 treaty is settled law with long practice behind it | Credit method means you pay the higher of the two rates, never the lower |
| No at-risk or job-creation requirement, unlike EB-5 | The mandatory Greek account creates FBAR and FATCA reporting |
| Greek property is a real asset you own outright | The CGT suspension transfers the gain to the IRS rather than saving it |

## Risks a US buyer should price before signing

- **Assuming the Greek suspension is a saving.** It is a timing question for Americans and the modelling
  should show the US liability in full.
- **Filing the first return before taking advice.** Ownership structure and the funding trail are
  cheaper to set up correctly than to unwind.
- **Holding through an entity without checking the US treatment.** A foreign entity holding foreign
  property can carry its own US information-return regime, and the wrong wrapper is expensive.
- **Relying on stale EB-5 figures** in any comparison, including ones published by advisers.
- **Forgetting the FBAR is separate.** It is not filed with the 1040 and missing it has its own penalty
  regime.

## Buyer scenarios where the American position actually bites

**The buyer who intends to sell within five years.** Model the disposal at US rates from the outset. The
Greek suspension changes the Greek bill and not the total, and a plan built on the headline is built on
somebody else's tax position.

**The buyer who intends to let.** Expect a Greek bill computed on gross rent and a US return computed on
net profit after depreciation, with a foreign tax credit reconciling them imperfectly. Budget for a
cross-border accountant annually, not once.

**The buyer who intends to move to Greece.** The Article 5A and 5B Greek regimes are attractive and they
do not switch off the US obligation. An American considering
[the non-dom route](/guides/greece-non-dom-tax-regime-5a-guide/) is layering a second system on top of
the first rather than replacing it.

**The buyer who wants the permit and nothing else.** This is the cleanest case: buy, hold, let
long-term, and accept that the compliance cost is the price of the passport you already have.

Involve a US cross-border accountant at the transfer rather than at the first tax return. Every position
on this page is cheaper to establish at purchase than to correct afterwards.
