**Quick answer:** Croatia does not operate a residence-by-investment programme. Property ownership there
confers no immigration status, and a third-country national who buys in Istria or Split still needs a
temporary-stay route based on work, family, study or self-employment. Greece grants a five-year renewable
residence permit for a qualifying property purchase, with no minimum stay requirement.

That is the whole comparison. Everything below is the consequence of it.

---

## A programme against no programme

The two countries are not offering the same product at different prices. Greece has a statutory
residence-by-investment route under Law 5100/2024; Croatia has property rights and a separate,
unconnected immigration system.

| | Greece | Croatia |
|---|---|---|
| Residence for buying property | Yes, statutory | No |
| Governing instrument | Law 5100/2024 | No equivalent |
| What a purchase gives you | A deed and a permit application | A deed |
| Immigration route for a buyer | The Golden Visa itself | Work, family, study or self-employment, judged on its own merits |
| Minimum stay to hold status | None | Depends entirely on the route relied on |
| Renewal basis | Continued ownership of the qualifying asset | Continued satisfaction of the stay route's conditions |

Croatia is in the EU and in Schengen, so an owner without residence status visits under the standard
90-days-in-180 rule like any other third-country national. That is worth stating plainly because Schengen
membership is frequently offered as though it were an immigration benefit of buying. It is not.

## Why the price difference misleads

Croatian coastal property is cheaper than the Greek €400,000 regional threshold in most of Istria and much
of Split, and considerably cheaper than the €800,000 Attica tier. A €280,000 Istria apartment is a real
purchase at a real price.

It secures ownership and nothing else. A buyer comparing €280,000 in Istria against €400,000 in Crete is
comparing an apartment with an apartment-plus-permit, and the €120,000 difference is the permit rather
than the building. Anyone whose objective is residence should treat the Croatian figure as irrelevant to
the decision, and anyone whose objective is a holiday home on the Adriatic should treat the Greek
threshold as irrelevant to theirs.

Dubrovnik prime stock breaks the pattern and prices at or above Greek regional levels, which removes even
the cost argument.

## What each country asks of the file

Greek and Croatian conveyancing are both notarial and both thorough, and neither is the risk here. The
divergence is in what happens after the deed.

A Greek Golden Visa file turns on four things: the tier that applies to the cadastral parcel, 120 square
metres of certified main usable area on one deed, a single title, and a fund trail arriving from abroad
through a Greek account in the buyer's name under Circular 1/2026. Ministry files fail on ENFIA arrears
and missing cadastre updates more than on anything exotic, which the
[due diligence guide](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/) works through.

A Croatian purchase file turns on title, boundaries and any agricultural or coastal restrictions, and then
stops. There is no ministry queue attached to it because there is no permit attached to it. What follows
instead is a separate immigration application with its own evidence, which the property does not support.

## Letting: both restrict, differently

Greece prohibits short-term tourist letting of the qualifying Golden Visa asset nationwide for the life of
the permit. Twelve-month residential leases are permitted, and that restriction is absolute rather than
municipal.

Croatia imposes no such rule, because there is no qualifying asset to restrict. Coastal owners register
seasonal tourism rentals under Croatian licensing, which is a normal and substantial part of Adriatic
ownership economics. That is a genuine advantage of Croatia for an income buyer and a genuine
disadvantage of Greece — and it exists precisely because the Croatian property carries no permit to
protect.

## Advantages and disadvantages, stated as a choice

| Greece | Croatia |
|---|---|
| A permit follows the purchase, with no minimum stay | A cheaper coastline with no immigration outcome |
| Family coverage runs on one qualifying investment | Family routes are separate immigration applications |
| Predictable statutory thresholds and process | Lower entry prices across Istria and most of Split |
| Short-term letting of the qualifying asset is banned | Seasonal tourism letting is licensed and normal |
| Renewal rests on continued ownership | Status rests on the underlying stay route, not the deed |

## Red flags specific to this comparison

- **Any material presenting Croatian property as a residence route.** There is no programme; ask which
  statute is being relied on and expect no answer.
- **Comparing headline prices across the two countries** as though they buy the same thing.
- **Assuming EU and Schengen membership confers rights on a non-resident owner.** It does not.
- **Budgeting once for a dual objective.** A buyer who wants Adriatic ownership and Greek residence needs
  two budgets, not one split.
- **Relying on Greek Golden Visa timelines** for a Croatian file that has no equivalent process.

## Buyer scenarios: which country answers which question

**Residence is the objective.** Greece, and the Croatian option does not arise. Choose the tier and read
the [tier map](/guides/greece-golden-visa-property-tiers-2026/) before the location.

**A cheaper Adriatic holiday home, with visits inside 90 days in 180.** Croatia, comfortably. The saving
against Greek thresholds is real and the immigration question never arises.

**Seasonal rental income is the objective.** Croatia, because Greek Golden Visa property cannot take it
and a non-qualifying Greek property is a different purchase from the one this page compares.

**Both, at once.** Two properties and two budgets. A €280,000 Istria apartment plus a €400,000 Cretan
deed is a coherent plan; one apartment doing both jobs is not.

## Croatia does not even sell ownership freely to everyone

The page has said Croatia grants no residence for buying. There is a second Croatian rule that catches
buyers earlier than that one, and it is the reason a comparison table of prices is misleading before it is
incomplete.

Croatian acquisition rights depend on who you are. Nationals of EU and EEA states buy on broadly the same
footing as Croatians, with agricultural and forest land treated separately. Third-country nationals do not
have that footing: acquisition rests on reciprocity between Croatia and the buyer's own country, and
where reciprocity applies the purchase requires consent from the Croatian Ministry of Justice before the
title transfers. Where no reciprocity exists, an individual simply cannot acquire.

The standard workaround is a Croatian company. A d.o.o. incorporated in Croatia is a domestic legal
person and acquires without the consent step, which is why so many Adriatic purchases by non-Europeans
run through one. That is a real solution and it is not free: it introduces Croatian corporate accounting,
annual filings, and a different tax treatment of both rental income and eventual disposal from the one an
individual owner would face.

So the honest sequence for a non-EU buyer is: establish whether you can own at all, then decide whether
you want to own personally or through a company, and only then look at prices. A Greek buyer's first
question is which tier applies to the parcel. A Croatian buyer's first question is whether their passport
permits the purchase in the first place.

## What each country charges to acquire and to hold

Croatia charges real estate transfer tax on resale purchases, and new-build sold by a VAT-registered
developer carries VAT instead of the transfer tax, which is the same either-or structure Greece runs and
one of the few genuine parallels between them. Croatia also introduced an annual real estate tax to
replace its old holiday-home charge, set within a statutory range by each municipality, which means an
Istrian coastal town and an inland one can charge materially different amounts on comparable properties.
Get the rate from the specific općina rather than a national average.

The Greek acquisition and holding stack is set out line by line in the
[cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-greece/) and is not repeated here. The point worth
carrying away is structural rather than arithmetic: the Greek total is higher and part of what it buys is
an immigration outcome, while the Croatian total is lower and buys a building. Setting the two totals
beside each other without naming that difference is the mistake this page exists to prevent, and it is
the mistake most comparison content makes.

Price the Croatian side through a Croatian lawyer. Nothing on a Greek site, including this one, is a
reliable source for Croatian transfer tax, municipal rates or company costs, and a buyer who infers them
from Greek figures will be wrong in both directions.
