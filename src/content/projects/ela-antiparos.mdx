**Quick answer:** ELA Antiparos is a Solena boutique scheme on Antiparos, which qualifies for the
€400,000 Golden Visa tier rather than €800,000 because its registered population of roughly 1,200 sits
below the 3,100-resident island line in Law 5100/2024. That halves the capital required and creates the
scheme's central problem: the small permanent population that produces the lower tier is also the only
tenant pool a twelve-month lease can draw on, and twelve months is the only letting a qualifying deed
may carry. Solena publishes no unit pricing. None of this substitutes for a Greek lawyer and an engineer on the specific unit.

## The tier discount and the income problem are the same fact

Antiparos qualifies at €400,000 rather than €800,000 because its registered population, around 1,200,
sits below the 3,100-resident line Law 5100/2024 draws through the islands. That halves the capital a
Golden Visa file needs, and it is the strongest single argument for the scheme.

It is also the reason the asset is hard to let. The same small permanent population that puts Antiparos
in the lower tier is the population a twelve-month lease has to be signed with — and twelve months is
the only letting a qualifying deed may carry, because short-term tourist letting of that deed is
prohibited nationwide for the life of the permit.

So the €400,000 saving is not free. It is paid for with the income, and the trade is worth stating
plainly before anything else on the page: on Antiparos the tier is generous and the yield is not, and
both come from the same census line.

## What the island actually earns, and why the permit cannot touch it

Antiparos generates money in summer. Its economy is a season — arrivals by ferry through Paros, a few
concentrated months, and a rental market priced for weeks rather than years. A non-qualifying property
here can be let short-term under the ΑΜΑ registry and can earn accordingly.

The qualifying property cannot. That single restriction removes the island's principal income stream
from the one asset the buyer is committing €400,000 to, and leaves the long-let market underneath it.
That market exists — Paros and Antiparos long lets run in the region of 2.5% to 4.5% gross — but it is
priced for local households and seasonal workers, not for premium boutique stock, and premium stock is
what a Solena ΕΛΑ scheme is.

The structure that follows from this is common and worth naming: buyers who want both the permit and
island income hold two assets, a qualifying one let long-term and a separate non-qualifying one licensed
for short-term letting. That doubles the capital but separates the two regimes cleanly, and it is the
only compliant way to have both. The
[Golden Visa short-term rental ban](/guides/greece-golden-visa-no-short-term-rental/) sets out what the
prohibition covers and for how long.

## The 120 m² rule bites harder on a small island than the price does

At €400,000 the price test on Antiparos is comfortable. The size test is not, and on island boutique
stock it is where files fail.

Compact luxury is the product here: high specification, generous outdoor space, modest enclosed
footprint. That is a sensible thing to build on a small Cycladic island and an awkward thing to qualify
with, because the 120 square metres the law asks for is main usable area on the Electronic Building
Identity — the enclosed interior, with verandas, pergolas, external stairs, storage and pool terraces
all excluded.

| What island marketing counts | What the certificate counts |
|---|---|
| Covered outdoor living | No |
| Roof terrace or pergola | No |
| Storage and plant rooms | No |
| Two adjoining units sold together | No — one deed only |
| Enclosed interior on one title | Yes, and this alone must reach 120 m² |

A scheme of small high-specification units may have nothing in it that qualifies at any price. That is
not a criticism of the architecture; it is a mismatch between what sells on a Cycladic island and what a
residence permit measures, and it is checked with one document. Get the enclosed area certified before
any non-refundable payment, as the [120 m² rule guide](/guides/greece-golden-visa-120-square-meter-rule/)
explains.

## Island purchases add checks a mainland deed does not need

Coastal Cycladic sites carry constraints that do not arise in Athens and that a buyer cannot see from a
render:

- **The aigialos setback.** The foreshore zone is state property with a defined boundary, and where that
  boundary has not been formally delimited near a site, construction close to the water is exposed.
- **Archaeological clearance.** The Cyclades sit under Ephorate oversight and a find during excavation
  stops work, sometimes permanently.
- **Forestry classification.** Scrub-covered hillside can be recorded on the forest map in ways an
  agricultural plot is not.
- **Water and waste.** Small islands run seasonal supply constraints and private treatment systems,
  which are running costs and permitting questions rather than amenities.
- **Access.** Every inspection, every trade and every subsequent repair arrives by ferry through Paros,
  which is a cost line and a scheduling constraint on a build.

None of these is exotic and all are resolvable, but they add time and legal cost to an island file that
a mainland resale does not carry.

## What Solena has not published, and what to ask for

Solena does not publish unit-level pricing for island ΕΛΑ schemes, so eligibility here cannot be
established from public material. The written quote needs to carry, per unit: the deed price, the
certified enclosed area, confirmation that the sale is one title, and the tax treatment — 24% VAT as a
first delivery or 3.09% transfer tax, confirmed by your own lawyer.

Two further questions are specific to island boutique product. Ask whether any part of the scheme is
permitted or marketed for tourist use, because a tourist-use encumbrance on the title conflicts directly
with the Golden Visa letting restriction. And ask what the service or management arrangement is, because
a rental pool over the qualifying unit is the same conflict wearing different clothes.

## Everything reaches this island through Paros

Antiparos has no airport and no direct connection to the mainland. Arrivals route through Paros and cross
by ferry from Pounta, a short hop that nonetheless makes every guest transfer a two-leg journey and puts
the shoulder-season schedule outside anyone's control.

The same channel carries the build. Materials, plant and trades arrive the same way, which is a real
input into an off-plan delivery date on a small island and a question worth putting to the developer
directly rather than inferring from a Cycladic average.

## Who should buy here, and who should not

This suits a buyer who wants a Cycladic house, is content to use it, and treats the permit as the reason
the purchase is structured at €400,000 rather than the reason for the purchase. On those terms Antiparos
is one of the cheapest routes to a Greek residence permit that also produces something you would want to
own.

It suits poorly anyone underwriting income. A buyer who needs the asset to earn should compare the same
€400,000 against Cretan city stock, where the tenant is resident rather than seasonal and the long-let
market is real all year — the [Crete guide](/guides/crete-property-investment-guide/) sets out that side.
And a buyer weighing the neighbouring island should read
[Paros and Antiparos](/guides/paros-antiparos-property-investment/), because the tier boundary between
them is a municipal population line and not a ferry distance.
