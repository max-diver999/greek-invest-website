**Quick answer:** Kalamata qualifies at €400,000 and, unlike the coastal markets that usually come with
that tier, it has a year-round tenant. It is the administrative and commercial capital of Messinia with a
working port, a hospital, courts, a university presence and a domestic airport carrying seasonal
international routes, and Athens is two and a half to three hours away on the motorway. The twelve-month
residential lease that a Golden Visa asset is limited to is an ordinary transaction here.

That is the whole argument for the city, and it is an argument about the tenant rather than about the
price.

## The permit's only allowed income is the income this city actually produces

Every Golden Visa asset in Greece is subject to the same restriction: short-term tourist letting of the
qualifying property is prohibited nationwide for the life of the permit, and a twelve-month residential
lease is the only compliant income.

On most Greek coastlines that restriction removes the income the place generates and replaces it with one
it does not. Kalamata is the exception in this collection, because its rental demand is not seasonal
visitors. It is hospital staff, court and administrative employees, port and logistics workers, teachers,
students and the households attached to them — people who need a lease that runs through February.

| Market | €400,000 tier | Compliant twelve-month tenant |
|---|---|---|
| Kalamata | Yes | Yes, from a working urban economy |
| [Antiparos](/guides/paros-antiparos-property-investment/) and the small Cyclades | Yes | Minimal — the registered population is around a thousand |
| [Mykonos](/areas/mykonos-property-investment/), [Santorini](/areas/santorini-property-investment/) | No, €800,000 | Thin, and priced far below the purchase |
| [Rhodes](/areas/rhodes-property-investment/) | Yes | Yes, but a non-EU buyer needs Defence clearance first |

The table is the reason to consider Kalamata at all. A buyer who wants the coast, the lower tier and an
asset that earns something while it supports a permit has a short list, and this city is on it.

## What the port and the airport actually do for an owner

Kalamata's port is commercial and agricultural rather than a cruise terminal of the scale that reshapes a
seafront. Its practical effect on property is employment and freight, not tourist footfall, which is
precisely why the tenant base is stable.

The airport matters differently. It carries domestic traffic year-round and seasonal international routes
to northern Europe, which supports both personal use and the second-home market — but seasonal routes are
commercial decisions that change, and a purchase underwritten on direct flights from a particular city is
underwritten on something a carrier can withdraw. The motorway to Athens is the connection that does not
move.

## Where the money goes, and what €400,000 clears

The Messinian coast is not one market. Kalamata city, the seafront, the surrounding villages and the
resort corridor toward Costa Navarino price very differently, and only some of them let year-round.

- **City centre and the administrative quarter** — the deepest long-let demand, the least holiday appeal,
  and the most reliable place to underwrite a Golden Visa asset.
- **The seafront and the marina strip** — a premium paid for aspect, with mixed year-round and seasonal
  tenancy.
- **Villages inland and along the coast** — the lowest entry per square metre and the thinnest tenant
  pool, where a twelve-month lease can be genuinely hard to sign.
- **The Costa Navarino halo** — proximity pricing that reflects a resort rather than the local market;
  [Costa Navarino](/areas/costa-navarino-property-investment/) is a different proposition entirely and is
  covered separately.

At Messinian price levels €400,000 buys well above the 120 square metre statutory minimum in every one of
these except the top of the seafront band, so the size test is rarely the binding constraint. What binds
instead is title.

## The cheap square metres are cheap because of the paperwork

This is the Peloponnese, and the discount that makes the region attractive comes attached to a specific
set of problems. Inherited village and olive-country stock frequently carries an incomplete ownership
chain, unregistered heirs, plots that overlap the forest map, and additions built over decades without
permit.

Three timescales are worth memorising before making an offer:

1. **Cadastre gaps** — an unresolved ownership chain or an unknown-owner entry runs 12 to 24 months
   through the courts.
2. **Forest-registry overlap** — a plot partly classified as forest freezes a sale until the Forestry
   Service rules, which takes 18 to 36 months.
3. **Unpermitted additions** — some are legalisable under Law 4495/2017 against a fine and some are not,
   and an un-legalisable extension counts toward neither the price nor the certified area.

City-centre apartment stock is largely free of all three, which is another reason a Golden Visa buyer
here should be looking at the city rather than the postcard. The
[Peloponnese guide](/guides/peloponnese-property-investment-guide/) covers the regional title picture in
full.

## Buying for a permit against buying for a life

The two purposes point at different streets in Kalamata, and conflating them is the most common mistake
made here.

A permit-first buyer wants an apartment in the administrative quarter with clean title, certified area
comfortably above 120 square metres, and a tenant already in the building. A life-first buyer wants the
seafront or a village with a view, will accept a thinner letting market, and should not be modelling
income at all.

Both are legitimate. What does not work is buying the second and underwriting the first, because the
village house that charmed you in May has no twelve-month tenant in November, and the permit does not
allow you to let it to the people who would want it.

## Two buyer profiles, and what each should ignore

| | Permit-first buyer | Life-first buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Where to look | City centre and the administrative quarter | Seafront, marina strip, or a village |
| Target size | Comfortably above 120 m² certified | Whatever the house is |
| Income assumption | A twelve-month lease already achievable | None — do not model it |
| Title risk tolerance | Low: apartment stock with clean cadastre | Higher, with 12 to 36 month resolution times accepted |
| What to ignore | The view | The yield table |

### Risks and red flags in a Messinian file

Three problems recur and all are about paperwork rather than price. An incomplete ownership chain on
inherited stock runs 12 to 24 months through the courts. A plot overlapping the forest map freezes a sale
for 18 to 36 months until the Forestry Service rules. And unpermitted additions may or may not be
legalisable under Law 4495/2017, with the un-legalisable ones counting toward neither the price nor the
certified area. City apartment stock avoids all three, which is why a permit-first buyer belongs there.

## What a €400,000 Kalamata purchase costs to complete

The threshold is the qualifying investment rather than the capital required, and Messinia adds nothing
exotic to the standard Greek stack. Transfer tax at 3.09% on a €400,000 taxable value is about €12,360.
Notary runs €4,000 to €6,000, lawyer €4,000 to €6,000, land registry €1,900 to €2,600, and the engineer's
certificate €800 to €1,500. Golden Visa state fees add €2,000 per adult and €1,000 per dependent under
21.

That puts a realistic all-in figure between €428,000 and €440,000 for a single applicant, and closer to
€445,000 for a family of four. Kalamata sits roughly 240 kilometres from Athens by motorway, which is the two and a half to three hours quoted above, and the drive rather than the flight is what makes the second-home market work in winter. Where a property is a first delivery from a developer, 24% VAT can apply
instead of the transfer tax, which is a determination for your own lawyer rather than the seller.
