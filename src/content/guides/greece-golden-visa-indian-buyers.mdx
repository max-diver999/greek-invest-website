the Liberalised Remittance Scheme a resident individual may remit up to USD 250,000 per financial year,

the funding structure before they solve the property, and the structure has to survive both FEMA scrutiny
and the Greek requirement under Circular 1/2026 that the money arrive in the buyer's own name.

## The LRS cap is per person, per financial year, and the year ends on 31 March

The Reserve Bank of India permits a resident individual to remit up to USD 250,000 in a financial year
under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme. The Indian financial year runs from 1 April to 31 March, and
the cap does not accrue: unused headroom does not carry forward.

At prevailing rates USD 250,000 falls well short of a €400,000 deed and further short of €800,000. That
single arithmetic fact governs the whole transaction.

| Route | How it works | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| Split across two financial years | Remit before 31 March, remit again after 1 April | The seller must agree to wait; the timetable stalls |
| Pool family remittances | Several resident individuals each remit within their own cap | Every remitter must appear on the title, which changes the Golden Visa file |
| Fund from existing offshore assets | Money already held abroad legitimately is outside LRS | Requires a clean, documented offshore source |

## Pooling changes who owns the property, and that reaches the permit

The pooling route is the one most often chosen and the one most often mis-executed. If three family
members each remit within their own LRS cap, Greek and Indian requirements pull in opposite directions:
Circular 1/2026 requires the purchase price to arrive in an account in the **buyer's** name, and FEMA
requires each remitter's money to be used for that remitter's own purpose.

The consequence is that the people who funded the purchase generally need to appear on the deed. That is
manageable, and it interacts with the Golden Visa in ways worth understanding before the money moves:
the permit rests on one qualifying property, the thresholds are tested on the deed as a whole, and joint
ownership among family members is not the same thing as one applicant with dependants. Establish with a
Greek lawyer, in writing and before the first remittance, whose names go on the title and which of them
is the applicant.

## Tax Collected at Source is a cash-flow item, not a tax

TCS applies to LRS remittances above ₹10 lakh in a financial year, at 20% for purposes other than
education and medical treatment. It is collected at the point of remittance by the authorised dealer
bank.

TCS is not an additional tax; it is credited against your Indian income tax liability and can be claimed
back through your return. But it is withheld at the moment the money leaves, so a buyer sending money for
a Greek deposit needs the cash for both the deposit and the TCS at the same time, and gets the TCS back
much later. Budget it as working capital rather than as a cost.

## FEMA compliance is the risk that outlasts the purchase

Exceeding the LRS limit, or structuring around it, exposes the remitter to scrutiny and penalties under
the Foreign Exchange Management Act. Two patterns attract attention specifically: remittances routed
through third parties who are not related to the purchase, and remittances declared under one LRS purpose
code and applied to another.

The documentation to keep from the outset is the A2 form and purpose code for each remittance, the bank
advice, the Greek notarial deed showing the consideration, and the correspondence establishing who paid
what. A Greek file is closed once the permit issues; an Indian file can be reopened for years.

## Double taxation, and what the treaty actually does

India and Greece have a double tax treaty. Greek rental income is taxed in Greece first at 15% to 45% on
gross rent with only a flat 5% repairs allowance, and an Indian resident then reports the same income in
India and claims credit for the Greek tax paid.

Two practical notes. India taxes on residence, so an Indian who becomes non-resident changes the position
entirely, and the residency tests are day-count based and specific. And the credit is against Indian tax
on the same income, so where the Greek charge is higher the excess is generally not recoverable.

## Bringing the money home is not the mirror image of sending it

Everything on this page so far describes capital leaving India. The return journey has its own rules, and
Indian buyers routinely plan the first without planning the second.

Start with what you hold. Once the remittance has left under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme and been
applied to the purpose declared on the A2 form, you own a foreign asset. That asset has to be disclosed
on your Indian return in the schedule for foreign assets, every year, whether or not it produces income
and whether or not any tax is due on it. The disclosure obligation is separate from the tax obligation
and it does not switch off because the property is empty.

Then the sale. Proceeds of a foreign asset acquired under LRS are yours, and how they are treated depends
on your residential status at the time. A resident individual who sells and does not reinvest is expected
to bring the funds back within the period the Reserve Bank prescribes rather than leave them sitting
abroad indefinitely — leaving sale proceeds parked in a foreign account because the exchange rate is
unfavourable is a decision with a compliance dimension, not just a commercial one. Someone who has become
non-resident by then is in an entirely different regime, and the answer changes with them.

Residential status is therefore the first question in any exit planning conversation, not a detail to
confirm later. It is determined by day counts and by the specific tests in Indian law, and it can change
between the year you agree a sale and the year you complete it.

The capital gain is the third piece. A resident is taxed in India on worldwide capital gains, so a gain on
the Greek property is reportable in India even in a period when Greek capital gains tax on property is
suspended and nothing is payable in Greece. A suspension in one country is not an exemption in the other,
and the India-Greece treaty allocating rights over immovable property does not make an Indian resident's
gain disappear from an Indian return. Model the Indian charge when you model the exit, not after you have
agreed a price.

Rental income follows the same pattern as the gain. A resident is taxed in India on the Greek rent as it
arises, with credit for the Greek tax under the treaty, and the Indian return reports it whether or not
the money is ever brought back to India.

Gift transactions inside the family carry their own Indian treatment and are not a way around the LRS
cap, since a remittance funded by one person and applied for another's benefit is judged on substance.

Succession is the piece nobody models at all. If the Greek property passes to Indian heirs, they inherit
a foreign asset with Indian disclosure obligations of their own, alongside the Greek inheritance tax and
the Greek notarial acceptance procedure this site covers separately. Heirs who have never dealt with
foreign-asset reporting acquire that obligation with the property.

The single most useful thing to do about all of this is administrative rather than clever: keep the A2
forms, the bank advices, the TCS certificates, the purchase deed and the exchange rates in one place from
the first remittance. Every question above is easy to answer with those documents and expensive to answer
without them, and reconstructing them from Greek and Indian records a decade later is worse than either.

## Advantages and disadvantages for an Indian buyer

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| A treaty exists, so Greek tax paid is creditable in India | LRS caps a single remitter well below either threshold |
| Residence-based taxation, so relocation genuinely changes the position | The financial year boundary dictates the purchase timetable |
| Family pooling is a legitimate and commonly used route | Pooling puts multiple names on the deed, which reaches the permit |
| The Greek permit requires no minimum stay | TCS at 20% withholds cash long before it is refunded |

## Risks an Indian buyer should price before signing

- **Signing a preliminary agreement with a completion deadline the LRS timetable cannot meet.**
- **Pooling without a Greek lawyer's written view** on whose names go on the title and who the applicant
  is.
- **Treating TCS as a fee** rather than as withheld cash that has to be funded twice.
- **Mismatched purpose codes** between what was declared at remittance and what the money did.
- **Assuming the Greek tax is the whole tax**, when the Indian return brings the same income back with
  only a credit for what Greece took.

## Buyer scenarios where the Indian position bites

**One buyer, €400,000, no offshore funds.** The purchase spans two financial years by necessity. Negotiate
the completion date around 31 March explicitly, and expect to pay a premium for a seller's patience.

**A family funding jointly.** The cleanest route on cash flow and the most complicated on title. Resolve
the ownership structure before the first remittance, because unwinding a deed is far more expensive than
drafting one.

**An NRI with existing offshore assets.** The simplest case, since money already held abroad legitimately
is outside LRS entirely. The documentation burden shifts from RBI compliance to satisfying Greek
source-of-funds requirements, which the
[proof of funds guide](/guides/golden-visa-greece-bank-transfer-proof-funds/) covers.

## A worked remittance timetable

The abstract constraint becomes concrete quickly. Assume a €400,000 deed, an all-in requirement near
€430,000 once transfer tax at 3.09% of about €12,360 and the notary, lawyer, registry and engineer lines
are added, and a single resident individual remitting under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme.

| Step | Timing | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Reservation and preliminary agreement | Month 0 | Typically 10% of price, funded within the current year's LRS headroom |
| First LRS tranche | Before 31 March | Up to USD 250,000, less 20% TCS withheld above Rs 10 lakh |
| Second LRS tranche | After 1 April | Balance, within the new financial year's cap |
| Notarial deed and registration | Month 3 to 6 | Full consideration must have arrived in the buyer's own Greek account |
| Golden Visa file | After the deed | 12 to 18 months to the residence card |

Two things the table makes visible. The completion date cannot be earlier than the start of the second
financial year unless another funding route is used, so a seller has to be willing to wait several months
between agreement and deed. And the TCS withheld on the way out is cash you do not have during the
purchase, recoverable only through a later Indian return, so the working-capital requirement exceeds the
purchase price by a meaningful margin.

A family pooling three individual caps compresses the timetable to a single financial year and moves the
complexity onto the title instead, which is why the ownership question has to be settled with a Greek
lawyer before the first rupee leaves.
