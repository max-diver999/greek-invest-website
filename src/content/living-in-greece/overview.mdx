**Quick answer:** to live in Greece rather than visit it, a non-EU national needs a
residence permit, because visa-free presence is capped at 90 days in any rolling
180-day period across the whole Schengen area. The permit routes divide by income
source: pension, remote employment, independent means, or investment through the
Golden Visa. Two preferential tax regimes are worth checking before you move, the
7 percent flat rate for foreign pensioners and the €100,000 non-dom charge for high
foreign incomes. Property comes after the permit question, not before it.

Most guides to living in Greece are written as a list of pleasant things followed by
a list of mild complaints. That is not useless, but it answers a question nobody has.
The people who actually move here are deciding between countries, and they need to
know which permit they qualify for, what it costs them in tax, and which parts of the
system will take longer than they expect.

This page is the structural version. Where a number is statutory it is cited to the
law that sets it.

## The 90-day ceiling, and why it is the starting point

A non-EU national may be present in the Schengen area for 90 days in any rolling
180-day period without a residence permit, under Article 6 of the Schengen Borders
Code. Three things about that rule catch people out.

It is **area-wide, not country-wide**. Days in Portugal, Italy or Croatia count
against the same 90. Buying a house in Greece does not create a separate Greek
allowance.

It is **rolling, not annual**. The window moves with you, so the calculation is not
"90 days per calendar year" and cannot be reset by flying home for a weekend.

It is **a ceiling on visits, not a status**. You cannot be resident in Greece on it,
cannot become tax resident through it, and cannot use it as a bridge while an
application is pending. The
[how long you can stay guide](/guides/how-long-can-you-stay-greece-golden-visa/)
works through how the count interacts with a residence permit once you hold one.

Everything else on this page assumes you intend to exceed that ceiling, which means
a permit.

## Which permit, by what funds you

Greece does not have one immigration route. It has several, and they sort by where
your money comes from rather than by where you want to live.

| If your income is | The route is | What it turns on |
|---|---|---|
| A foreign pension | Financially independent person, often with the 7% tax election | Proven regular pension income and health cover |
| Remote work for a non-Greek employer | Digital nomad visa and permit | Employment or client contracts outside Greece, plus an income floor |
| Savings and investments, no employment | Financially independent person | Demonstrable passive income and health cover |
| A property purchase | Golden Visa | The investment threshold for the location |
| A Greek employer | Work permit | A job offer and a quota position |

The Golden Visa is the route this site covers in most depth, and it is also the
route most often chosen for the wrong reason. It is an investment permit: it grants
residence without requiring you to live in Greece, which suits someone who wants
optionality. If your intention is actually to move, the pension and independent-means
routes are usually cheaper and involve no property purchase at all. The
[Golden Visa hub](/golden-visa/) sets out the thresholds; the point here is that
buying a house is one route among several rather than the default.

## The two tax regimes worth checking before you move

Greek tax residence generally follows presence, and crossing 183 days in a year
brings worldwide income into scope. Two special regimes change that arithmetic, and
both have to be elected rather than granted automatically.

**The 7 percent pensioner regime.** Article 5B of Law 4172/2013 lets a foreign
pensioner who transfers tax residence to Greece elect a flat 7 percent rate on
worldwide income for a fixed period. What makes it notable is the scope: it covers
foreign-source income generally, not the pension alone. Conditions attach to prior
non-residence and to the source country, so it is checked against your own position.
The [7 percent pension regime guide](/guides/greece-7-percent-pension-tax-retirees/)
sets out the eligibility test.

**The non-dom regime.** Article 5A charges a flat €100,000 a year on foreign-source
income, regardless of the size of that income, and entry requires an investment of at
least €500,000 in Greek real estate, a business or securities. The flat charge is the
whole point: above a certain income it is dramatically cheaper than the ordinary
scale, and below that income it is dramatically worse. The
[non-dom guide](/guides/greece-non-dom-tax-regime-5a-guide/) shows where the
crossover sits.

| Regime | Charge | Entry condition |
|---|---|---|
| Article 5B, foreign pensioners | 7% flat on worldwide income | Transfer of tax residence, prior non-residence, qualifying pension |
| Article 5A, non-domiciled | €100,000 flat on foreign income | Investment of at least €500,000 in Greece |
| Ordinary Greek residence | Progressive scale on worldwide income | Default position past 183 days |

Neither regime is a reason to move. Both are reasons to time a move carefully, and
to take the tax advice before the removal quote rather than after.

## If you let a property while you are here

The rental income scale changed on 1 January 2026 under Law 5246/2025, and it changed
in a way that matters most to the middle of the range.

| Annual gross rent | Rate from 2026 |
|---|---|
| Up to €12,000 | 15% |
| €12,001 to €24,000 | 25% |
| €24,001 to €35,000 | 35% |
| Above €35,000 | 45% |

The 25 percent band is new, and income that previously sat in a lower band moved into
it. The other feature that surprises foreign landlords is the deduction: an individual
may set only a flat 5 percent for repairs and maintenance against gross rent. There is
no deduction for management, insurance, travel or the mortgage. The charge therefore
falls on something close to the gross figure rather than on profit, which is why yield
projections built on net-of-costs assumptions overstate the result.
The [rental income guide](/guides/greece-rental-income-tax-non-resident/) works
through the filing mechanics for a non-resident owner.

One further restriction applies if the property is also a Golden Visa asset: it may
only be let on a lease of twelve months or longer, and short-term tourist letting is
prohibited. That rule binds the property, not the owner, so it survives a change of
plans.

## Where foreigners actually settle, and how the three choices differ

Almost every foreign resident ends up in one of three places, and the difference
between them is not scenery. It is how much of the year the place operates at full
capacity, and how much administration you can do without leaving town.

| | Athens and the Riviera | Crete | The Ionian islands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year-round operation | Full, all services open | Full in the cities, seasonal in the villages | Seasonal outside the main towns |
| Administration on the spot | Every office, ministries included | Regional offices in Heraklion and Chania | Limited, often a ferry away |
| International schooling | Several established options | Limited, concentrated in the cities | Effectively none |
| Healthcare depth | Full hospital network, private and public | Regional hospitals in the two main cities | Local clinics, referrals off-island |
| Who it tends to suit | Working residents, families, anyone needing the bureaucracy nearby | People who want island character with a mainland service level | Second-home owners and retirees who accept the winter |

**Athens** is the practical answer for anyone still working, raising children, or
dealing with a permit application that will need several visits to the same building.
The Riviera suburbs give a coastal life inside a functioning city, which is a
combination the islands cannot offer.

**Crete** is the compromise that works most often. It is large enough to carry two
proper cities with hospitals and regional government, and it runs through the winter
rather than closing, while still being an island. That is why it absorbs more
long-term foreign residents than any other Greek island.

**The Ionian islands**, Corfu and Kefalonia in particular, are where the second-home
and retirement demand concentrates, and they carry the sharpest seasonal difference.
The summer version and the February version of Corfu are close to different places.
That suits people who came for the quiet and frustrates people who came for the
summer they remembered.

None of these is better. They fail for different reasons, and knowing which failure
you can tolerate is more useful than a ranking.

## What the pros-and-cons lists get right

The advantages that appear on every list are real and are not worth restating at
length. Climate, coastline, food, a slower default pace, a genuinely low crime rate
in most places people actually settle, and a healthcare system that works better than
its reputation once you are inside it.

Two are worth adding because they rarely appear:

**Distance.** Greece is a three to four hour flight from most of Western Europe and a
direct long-haul from the eastern United States in summer. For anyone keeping ties to
another country, the flight map does more for quality of life than any single local
amenity.

**Optionality inside the country.** The gap between Athens and a small island is not
just scenery, it is a different life with different costs and a different amount of
administration. Few countries of this size let you change your mind that completely
without changing your paperwork.

## What they get wrong, or leave out

**The administration is the hard part, and it does not stop.** The tax number, the
residence permit, the social security registration, the utility transfers and the
periodic bank compliance reviews each run on separate timetables and none of them
shares information with the others. Foreign residents who settle well are the ones
who treated this as a fixed cost of the move and paid an accountant, rather than the
ones who expected it to get easier after year one.

**Island life and mainland life are different products.** Winter on a small island
means a reduced ferry timetable, some services closed, and a much smaller pool of
tradespeople. That is a feature for some people and a reason to leave for others, and
it is not visible on an August viewing trip. Anyone considering an island should see
it in February before committing.

**Banking is slower than you expect.** Opening an account as a non-resident is a
compliance exercise rather than a formality, and it recurs: accounts get reviewed and
documents get requested again. Build it into timetables rather than treating it as a
one-off.

**Tax residence arrives whether you plan for it or not.** Crossing 183 days makes you
Greek tax resident, and that is a status with consequences for worldwide income,
reporting and sometimes for the tax treatment of assets in your home country. The
people who get hurt are not the ones who chose the wrong regime, they are the ones
who did not realise they had chosen at all.
