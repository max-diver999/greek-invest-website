**Quick answer:** the Greek digital nomad permit requires around €3,500 a month in
income earned from outside Greece, plus roughly 20% for a spouse and 15% per
dependent child, evidenced by contracts and payment history. Since February 2026 the
application is lodged at a Greek consulate before you travel. A 50% exemption on
qualifying income is available for up to seven years by separate application to the
tax authorities. The permit does not decide your tax position: presence past 183 days
does that, and it is why the exemption exists.

The two things that catch people on this route are both about boundaries. Where the
income comes from, and where the tax lands. Neither is a question the visa
application itself answers.

## What the permit asks

| Requirement | What it means |
|---|---|
| Monthly income | About €3,500 for the main applicant |
| Spouse or partner | Roughly 20% on top |
| Each dependent child | Roughly 15% on top |
| Source of income | Work for clients or an employer outside Greece |
| Evidence | Contracts and payment history, not a single balance |
| Where you apply | A Greek consulate, before travelling, since February 2026 |

Those figures are published consistently across independent immigration advisories.
They are not read here from a Ministry or consular primary document, and consular
requirements vary by post and change, so treat them as planning numbers and confirm
with the consulate handling your file.

## The income has to come from outside Greece

This is the defining condition and it is narrower than it looks.

The permit exists for people whose work is performed in Greece but paid from
elsewhere. An employee of a foreign company, a freelancer with foreign clients, a
contractor billing abroad. What it does not cover is income earned from Greek
clients or a Greek employer, and taking Greek work changes the basis on which the
permit was granted rather than adding to it.

Two practical consequences follow. First, the evidence is relational rather than
numerical: a consulate is looking for contracts showing who pays you and where they
are, not simply for a bank balance of the right size. Second, anyone whose plan
includes eventually working locally should treat this permit as a temporary
arrangement rather than a route, and check the position before the local work starts.

The mirror image of this rule sits on the
[retirement route](/guides/greece-retirement-visa/), which requires passive income
and excludes employment income entirely. Between the two permits, almost every income
type is covered, but each excludes what the other requires, and the amount asked is
similar enough that composition rather than size usually decides which one applies.

## The 50% exemption, and what it is not

A person who transfers tax residence to Greece and qualifies can obtain a 50%
exemption on Greek-source employment or business income for up to seven years,
subject to registration and approval by the Greek tax authorities.

Three things about it are routinely misread.

**It is an application, not an entitlement.** Being granted the residence permit does
not grant the tax treatment. They are separate processes, decided by different
authorities, on different criteria.

**It has conditions attached**, including on prior non-residence and on an
undertaking to remain, and it runs for a defined period rather than indefinitely.

**It applies to the income the Greek system taxes**, which is a different set from
"all my income". Anyone with investment income, property income or a pension
alongside their work needs to know which parts the exemption touches and which parts
sit on the ordinary scale, and that is a question for a preparer rather than for a
visa page.

## Where the tax actually lands

The permit is an immigration document. It says nothing about tax residence, and
people conflate the two constantly.

Presence beyond 183 days in a calendar year generally makes you Greek tax resident
under Article 4 of Law 4172/2013, at which point worldwide income comes into the
Greek net unless a preferential regime or a double tax treaty provides otherwise.
That is the expected outcome of moving here on this permit rather than an accident,
and it is precisely why the 50% exemption exists as an incentive.

The expensive assumption is the opposite one: that a nomad visa somehow keeps you
outside the Greek system while you enjoy the weather. It does not, and [sequencing the move](/living-in-greece/moving/) sets out how the
183-day count interacts with the 90-day Schengen ceiling. For very high foreign incomes the
[non-dom regime](/guides/greece-non-dom-tax-regime-5a-guide/) is a different and
sometimes better answer, though its entry condition is an investment of at least
€500,000 in Greece.

Americans should note that US filing obligations continue regardless, so the outcome
is two tax positions running in parallel rather than one replacing the other.

## What changed in February 2026

Applications moved to consulates. Before that change it was possible to arrive on a
visa-free entry and apply from inside Greece; now the file has to be complete before
you travel.

The practical effect is on sequencing rather than on difficulty. Documents that
previously could be gathered while already in the country, with Greek help nearby,
now have to be assembled at home, apostilled and where necessary translated. Three to
four weeks for that chain is normal when nothing goes wrong.

Anyone reading a guide that describes an in-country application is reading a page
written before the change, which is a useful test of how current any other advice on
that page is likely to be.

## Where nomads actually base themselves in Greece

The permit is national and the experience is not. Three things vary enough to decide
whether the arrangement works: internet reliability, whether the place operates in
winter, and whether there is anyone else there doing the same thing.

**Athens** is the default for a reason. Full year-round infrastructure, the deepest
pool of coworking space, direct flights to most of Europe, and the ministries and
tax offices in the same city as you. The Riviera suburbs add coastline without giving
up any of it. It is also where the country's connectivity is best, which matters more
on this permit than on any other.

**Thessaloniki** is the cheaper city option with a large student population, a
functioning winter and good onward connections into the Balkans.

**Crete**, particularly Chania and Heraklion, is the island that works year-round,
because it carries two proper cities behind it. It is the usual answer for someone who
wants island life without accepting island infrastructure.

**Smaller islands** photograph best and suit this permit worst. Winter ferry
timetables, seasonal closures, a thinner pool of services and connectivity that varies
by village are all manageable for a holiday and awkward for a job. Anyone considering
one should test it in February on a working week rather than in August on a holiday.

The pattern is the same one that decides where retirees settle: the variable is not
scenery but whether the place operates when the tourists have gone. The
[overview of living in Greece](/living-in-greece/) compares the main options on
service levels rather than on price.

## What the permit does not solve

Three things stay your problem after the visa is granted, and each catches people.

**Your employer's position.** Working from another country can create obligations for
the company, on payroll, social security or permanent establishment, depending on the
jurisdictions involved. An employer who has not been asked is an employer who may
object later. This conversation belongs before the application, not after the move.

**Social security.** Which system you contribute to, and what happens to accrued
entitlements at home, is a separate question from both the permit and income tax. It
turns on the countries involved and on any agreement between them.

**Continuity.** The permit is a renewable temporary status built around a specific
income arrangement. If the arrangement changes, because a contract ends, a client base
becomes Greek, or the work stops being remote, the basis of the permit changes with
it. Anyone treating this as the first step of a decade-long plan should look at what
the later steps require rather than assuming one status flows into the next.

## Risks and red flags

**Assuming the visa settles the tax question.** It does not. Tax residence follows
presence, and the two are decided by different rules and different authorities.

**Assuming the 50% exemption is automatic.** It is a separate application with its
own conditions and approval step.

**Income evidence that shows amount but not source.** A consulate needs to see who
pays you and where they are. Statements alone do not establish that.

**Taking Greek clients once you are here.** It changes the basis of the permit rather
than supplementing it.

**Working from an outdated guide.** The February 2026 move to consulate applications
invalidated the sequence described by most pages written earlier.

**Underestimating the apostille chain.** It is the most common cause of a delayed
file and it now has to happen before departure rather than after arrival.

## The file, and the order to build it in

The application is granted on documents, so the work is assembling them in a sequence
where nothing expires while something slower is still running.

| Document | What it establishes | Lead time |
|---|---|---|
| Employment contract or client agreements | That the income comes from outside Greece | Yours to produce, but a client may need asking |
| Payment history | That it is real and recurrent | Bank statements across a run of months |
| Employer letter, where employed | That the arrangement is agreed | Depends entirely on the employer |
| Criminal record certificate | No disqualifying record | Weeks, plus apostille |
| Health insurance | Cover meeting the permit specification | Days, once you have read the specification |
| Proof of accommodation | Somewhere to live in Greece | A tenancy is cleanest |

Start with whatever depends on somebody else. A criminal record certificate has to be
issued, apostilled and often translated, and three to four weeks is normal when
nothing goes wrong. An employer letter can take longer than any government document,
because it usually requires an internal decision nobody has made yet.

The employer letter deserves its own note, because it is the item most likely to
derail a file and the one least under your control. A company that has never had an
employee working abroad may need legal advice before it will put anything in writing,
and that advice may take weeks and may come back negative.

Finish with whatever expires. Certificates and apostilles carry validity periods
measured in months, so a file assembled slowly can reach the consulate with its
earliest document already stale. That restarts one element rather than the whole
application, but it adds weeks each time.

The single most common cause of delay on this route is not the decision and not the
paperwork volume. It is starting the fast items first and discovering the slow ones
late.

## Buyer and applicant scenarios

**A UK freelancer billing UK clients €4,500 a month, moving alone.** Income clears
the threshold and the source qualifies. The file is assembled in the UK, apostilled
and lodged at the consulate. The tax question is the real work: presence past 183
days brings worldwide income into scope, so the 50% exemption application should be
prepared alongside rather than after.

**An American employed by a US company on €6,000 a month, with a spouse.** The
threshold rises by roughly 20% for the spouse, which is comfortably covered.
US filing continues in parallel with the Greek position, so the treaty and foreign
tax credit mechanics need a preparer who handles both jurisdictions. The employer
also needs to be comfortable with the arrangement, which is a conversation to have
before the application rather than after the move.

**A semi-retired consultant with €2,100 of client work and €2,450 of dividends.**
Neither route fits cleanly: the digital nomad permit looks at the active income and
the retirement route excludes it while counting the dividends. The realistic answer
is to establish which permit the income composition actually supports before choosing,
rather than assuming a combined figure of €4,550 qualifies for either.

**Someone buying property alongside the move.** The permit requires no purchase, and
buying is a separate decision with its own economics. If a purchase is part of the
plan for its own sake, the
[property investment guide](/guides/greece-property-investment-guide/) covers the
market and [where prices sit region by region](/property-for-sale/) covers the
geography.
