**Quick answer:** Greece is meaningfully cheaper than the EU average, but not evenly.
Eurostat's price level index for 2024 puts household final consumption at 86.0
against an EU27 average of 100, so overall consumer prices run about 14 percent below
the EU. Housing, water and energy sit at 70.5, which is where the saving is
concentrated. Food and non-alcoholic beverages sit at 105.8, above the EU average
rather than below it, and communication at 144.1 is the most expensive category
relative to the EU. Greece is cheap where you live and not cheap where you shop.

Most cost-of-living pages for Greece are built on crowdsourced averages: a number for
a cappuccino, a number for a one-bedroom apartment, a total at the bottom. Those
numbers move with whoever last submitted them and they cannot be checked.

This page is built on Eurostat's price level indices instead. They compare the cost
of a like-for-like basket between countries, they are published with a reference year
and a COICOP category code, and anyone can reproduce them. Every figure below is from
dataset `prc_ppp_ind`, indicator `PLI_EU27_2020`, geography Greece, reference year
2024, as published on 10 July 2025.

## What Greece actually costs against the EU average

| Category | COICOP | Greece (EU27 = 100) | Reading |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Household final consumption | E011 | **86.0** | The headline: 14% below the EU average |
| Education | A0110 | 59.0 | Cheapest category, narrow relevance |
| Housing, water, electricity, gas, fuels | A0104 | **70.5** | The real saving, close to 30% below |
| Health | A0106 | 81.8 | Meaningfully below |
| Recreation and culture | A0109 | 86.2 | Slightly below |
| Restaurants and hotels | A0111 | 86.4 | Slightly below |
| Transport | A0107 | 90.1 | Nearly at parity |
| Clothing and footwear | A0103 | 90.1 | Nearly at parity |
| Food and non-alcoholic beverages | A0101 | **105.8** | Above the EU average |
| Communication | A0108 | **144.1** | Far above the EU average |

Three of those rows do the work.

## The saving is in housing, and it is large

At 70.5, housing, water, electricity, gas and other fuels is the largest category
saving Greece offers and close to thirty percent below the EU average. It is also the
category that dominates most household budgets, which is why the headline figure of
86.0 lands where it does.

This is the number that makes the move work financially for retirees and remote
workers. It is not a small edge at the margins, it is the single biggest line in a
monthly budget coming in at roughly seven tenths of the EU price.

Two caveats belong with it. The index covers the cost of housing services generally
rather than the price of buying a specific property, and it is national rather than
local: Athens Riviera and a mainland town do not share a price level. And energy is
inside this category, which means the figure moves with energy markets in a way that
rent alone does not.

## Food is the surprise, and it goes the other way

At 105.8, food and non-alcoholic beverages in Greece cost more than the EU average.
Not less. This single figure contradicts the impression that almost every article on
the subject leaves.

The reasons are structural rather than temporary: import dependence for a large part
of the basket, logistics costs to islands and mountain regions, and a concentrated
retail sector. The practical consequence for anyone modelling a move is that the
weekly shop is not where the saving comes from, and a budget built on the assumption
that everything is cheaper will be wrong in a category people buy every week.

Restaurants and hotels at 86.4 sit below the EU average, which produces the
counter-intuitive combination people actually notice after a few months: eating out
is comparatively good value, and filling a fridge is not.

## Communication at 144.1 is the outlier

Mobile, broadband and related services in Greece are priced at 144.1 against an EU27
average of 100, making this the most expensive consumption category in the country
relative to the EU. For a remote worker whose entire income depends on a connection,
this is not a footnote. It is the one line item where moving to Greece costs
noticeably more than staying put, and it is worth pricing a specific package rather
than assuming a European average.

## Greece against the countries you are actually comparing it to

The useful comparison is rarely Greece against the EU average. It is Greece against
the other Mediterranean options on the same shortlist, measured the same way.

| Country | Household final consumption (EU27 = 100) |
|---|---:|
| Croatia | 76.3 |
| **Greece** | **86.0** |
| Portugal | 87.0 |
| Spain | 90.9 |
| Cyprus | 92.8 |
| Italy | 97.5 |
| Germany | 108.6 |
| France | 111.2 |

Two things fall out of that table.

**Greece is the cheapest of the mainstream options bar one.** Only Croatia comes in
lower. Greece sits below Portugal, which is the country it is most often compared
against on residence programmes, below Spain, below Cyprus and well below Italy. The
margin over Portugal is one index point, which is close enough to call a tie rather
than an advantage, but the margins over Spain, Cyprus and Italy are real.

**The gap against northern Europe is the size of the decision.** Someone moving from
France at 111.2 or Germany at 108.6 to Greece at 86.0 is looking at a shift of more
than twenty index points on the same basket. That is the number doing the work in
most relocation decisions, and it is larger than the difference between any two
Mediterranean countries on the list.

What the table does not settle is which of these countries is right, because price
level is one input among several. Portugal and Greece are separated by one index
point on cost and by a great deal on residence rules, tax regimes and property
markets. Anyone choosing between them on cost alone is choosing on a difference the
data cannot really support.

## If you are comparing from outside Europe

Eurostat's price level indices cover the EU, EFTA and candidate countries. They do
not cover the United States, so an American reader cannot answer "is Greece cheaper
than home" directly from this dataset, and neither can a Canadian or an Australian.

That is worth saying plainly, because a great many pages answer the question anyway
using a crowdsourced comparison and present it with the same confidence as an
official figure. The two are not the same kind of number.

What a reader outside Europe can do with this data is more useful than it first
looks. The index establishes Greece's position within Europe precisely: below
Portugal, Spain, Cyprus and Italy, and more than twenty points below France and
Germany. If you already have a sense of how your own costs compare to any one
European country you know well, this table places Greece against that reference.

For the remaining gap, the honest method is the same one that works anywhere.
Price your own housing from listings in the specific area you would live in, since
that is the largest line and the one with the widest local spread. Assume grocery
parity rather than a saving, because the index says food here runs above the European
average. And treat currency as a live variable rather than a constant, since a
dollar-denominated income against euro costs moves with the exchange rate by more
than most of the category differences discussed on this page.

## A note on the reference year

The figures above carry a 2024 reference year and were published on 10 July 2025.
Price level indices are compiled from surveys and released with a lag, which is the
cost of comparability: making a basket genuinely like-for-like across dozens of
countries takes time.

The lag matters for absolute impressions and matters much less for the comparisons
this page is built on. Relative positions between neighbouring European countries
move slowly, and the structural facts behind them, that Greek housing is cheap and
Greek food is not, are not artefacts of a single year.

## What this index cannot tell you

Being clear about the limits is more useful than another table.

| The index answers | The index does not answer |
|---|---|
| Is Greece cheaper than the EU average | What your monthly budget will be |
| Which categories carry the difference | What an apartment in a named suburb costs |
| How Greece compares to another EU country | How Athens compares to Crete or Corfu |
| Whether a saving is structural or marginal | What happens to island prices in winter |

Price level indices are published at national level. Greece has no official
island-by-island cost index, so any comparison between Athens, Crete and the Ionian
comes from listings and local knowledge rather than from a published statistic. Anyone
quoting a precise figure for a specific island is quoting an estimate, and it is worth
knowing that before treating it as data.

The seasonal point matters too. Small islands carry a logistics premium that widens
outside the tourist season, when supply chains thin out and fewer suppliers compete.
An August impression of island prices is not a February reality, which is one more
reason to see a place in winter before committing to it.

## The part of the number most comparisons miss

Cost of living is the smaller half of what a move actually costs. Tax is the other
half, and for many foreign residents it is the larger one.

| Position | Effect on a foreign resident |
|---|---|
| Article 5B, foreign pensioners | Flat 7% on worldwide income for a fixed period |
| Article 5A, non-domiciled | Flat €100,000 on foreign income, entry needs €500,000 invested in Greece |
| Ordinary Greek residence past 183 days | Progressive scale on worldwide income |
| Letting a property here | 15%, 25%, 35% and 45% bands from 2026, with only a flat 5% deduction |

A pensioner electing the
[7 percent regime](/guides/greece-7-percent-pension-tax-retirees/) can move their
after-tax position by more than any saving on rent. Someone crossing into Greek tax
residence without planning it can move it the other way. The
[non-dom guide](/guides/greece-non-dom-tax-regime-5a-guide/) shows where the flat
charge becomes cheaper than the scale.

If a property is part of the plan, two recurring costs belong in the same budget:
[ENFIA](/guides/enfia-property-tax-greece/), the annual property tax assessed on
objective value, and the
[rental income scale](/guides/greece-rental-income-tax-non-resident/) if you let it.

## How to build a number you can trust

Four steps, in order, and none of them involves a crowdsourced average.

**Start with the index.** It tells you whether Greece is cheaper than where you are
now, and by roughly how much. That is a real answer to a real question, and it is the
only part of this that is officially published.

**Price your own housing, locally.** Rent listings for the specific area, not a
national figure. This is the largest line and the one with the widest local spread.

**Adjust food upward, not downward.** Whatever you spend now on groceries, assume
parity or slightly more rather than a saving.

**Add the tax position.** Which regime, elected when, and what happens to income that
is currently taxed somewhere else. For most people this line is larger than the
difference in the shopping.

The result is a budget rather than a comparison, and it is the only version that
survives contact with a first year of actually living here. For where those trade-offs
land differently by location, the
[overview of living in Greece](/living-in-greece/) compares Athens, Crete and the
Ionian on service levels rather than on price, and the
[property investment guide](/guides/greece-property-investment-guide/) covers the
purchase side if buying is part of the plan.
