**Quick answer:** Mykonos is an €800,000 Golden Visa market with a 120 m² certified-area requirement and
a ban on short-term letting of the qualifying deed. What separates it from every other Greek market on
this site is that its illiquidity is seasonal rather than general: buyers arrive in spring and autumn, so
an owner who needs to sell in November is marketing into an empty room for six to eighteen months. Plan
the hold around that window before planning anything else.

Mykonos is the most internationally recognised address in the Cyclades and the one where the gap between
what a property earns and what it costs is widest. Everything below follows from a single structural
fact: the island runs on roughly four months of the year, and both of its markets — lettings and
resales — run on the same four.

---

## The exit has a season, and it is not the season you are picturing

Most illiquidity arguments about Greek islands are about depth: too few buyers, too long to sell. On
Mykonos the buyer pool is genuinely deep and international. The constraint is *when* it shows up.

Transactions cluster in spring and autumn — buyers who came in summer, decided, and returned to
transact. Outside those windows the market thins to almost nothing, and a trophy asset listed in
November can carry a six-to-eighteen-month marketing period before it meets the next spring cohort.

That has three consequences a purchase model should carry explicitly:

- **A forced sale is expensive here in a way it is not in Athens.** An owner who has to exit on someone
  else's timetable exits into the wrong month, and the discount is the price of the calendar rather than
  of the property.
- **Carrying costs run through the dead window.** ENFIA, common charges, insurance and maintenance
  continue for the six to eighteen months the asset is on the market.
- **The realistic hold is longer than the brochure implies.** Buying in one spring and selling in another
  is a multi-year plan by construction.

Resale depth on Mykonos still exceeds most small Cycladic islands. It trails Athens by a distance, and
the difference is a timing problem rather than a demand one.

## Why the yield is 2.5 to 4.0% when the summer rates look enormous

The nightly rates quoted for Mykonos villas are real and they are irrelevant to a Golden Visa asset.
Short-term tourist letting of the qualifying property is prohibited nationwide for the life of the
permit, so the only income that deed may earn is a twelve-month residential lease.

The arithmetic that follows is unforgiving. Purchase prices on prime coastal stock are set by what an
international summer buyer will pay. Long-term rents are set by what a resident on Mykonos can pay. Those
two numbers are not related to each other, and the ratio between them is the 2.5% to 4.0% gross that this
market actually produces.

| What sets the number | Priced by |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | International summer demand for a scarce address |
| Short-term nightly rate | Peak-season tourism, unavailable on a qualifying deed |
| Long-term monthly rent | The island's permanent population and its wages |
| Golden Visa income | The third line only |

The structure that resolves this is two assets rather than one: a qualifying property let long-term for
the permit, and a separate non-qualifying property licensed under the ΑΜΑ registry for seasonal letting.
It doubles the capital and separates the two regimes cleanly, which is the only compliant way to hold
both. The [short-term rental ban](/guides/greece-golden-visa-no-short-term-rental/) sets out exactly what
the prohibition covers.

## Where €800,000 stops clearing 120 square metres

Mykonos is in the €800,000 tier, so the price test is met by any purchase serious enough to be
considered. The size test is the one that fails, and it fails at a specific point on the price curve.

| Indicative €/m² | What €800,000 buys | Against the 120 m² floor |
|---|---|---|
| €4,500 | ~178 m² | Comfortable |
| €6,000 | ~133 m² | Clears with modest headroom |
| €7,500 | ~107 m² | Fails |
| €8,000 | ~100 m² | Fails |

The number to watch is the certified enclosed area, not the marketed one. On Cycladic stock the gap
between them is wide: covered terraces, pergolas, external stairs, storage and pool decks are all part of
how the house lives and none of them counts toward the 120 square metres of κύρια χρήση recorded on the
Electronic Building Identity. A house described as 150 m² can certify at 105.

Buyers at the top of the price band therefore face a choice rather than a calculation — the seafront
position or the permit — and it is worth making that choice before a deposit rather than after an
engineer's visit. The [120 m² rule](/guides/greece-golden-visa-120-square-meter-rule/) explains what the
certificate counts.

## Buying out of plan on a small island, and what that adds

Most Mykonos stock a foreign buyer looks at is built ektos schediou, outside the town plan, on a plot
rather than a street. That changes what is being purchased: the plot's legal status is the asset, and the
house sits on top of it.

Five checks belong in the file before any payment:

1. **Buildability of the plot** under the out-of-plan rules and any derogation the existing structure
   relied on.
2. **The aigialos boundary**, the state-owned foreshore, and whether it has been formally delimited near
   the site.
3. **Ephorate clearance**, since the Cyclades sit under archaeological oversight and a find during works
   halts them.
4. **Water and waste**, because the island runs seasonal supply constraints and most out-of-plan houses
   depend on private tanks and treatment.
5. **Legal road access**, which on plots assembled from agricultural land is frequently informal and
   occasionally absent.

None of these is unusual and all take time. Budget the calendar as well as the fees, and read the
[due diligence guide](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/) alongside a local engineer.

## Every trade on this island is priced by the season

The holding cost of a Mykonos property is set by a labour market that exists for four months and is
scarce for all of them. Builders, plumbers, electricians, pool technicians, gardeners and cleaners are
fully committed from May to September at rates the hospitality industry sets, and an owner competing for
that time is competing with hotels and villa operators whose revenue justifies paying whatever it takes.

The practical shape of this is specific and it catches people. Emergency work in August is expensive and
slow, because everyone capable of doing it is already booked. Planned work is cheapest and most available
in winter, when much of the island's workforce has left and those who remain want the contract. Anything
that must happen between June and September should be scheduled in February.

The second half of it is that Mykonos property is maintenance-heavy in the first place. Salt air, strong
and constant wind, and intense summer sun degrade external finishes, glazing, metalwork and pool plant
faster than a mainland equivalent, and a property standing empty for eight months does not stop needing
attention. It needs a caretaker, and the caretaker is a real annual cost rather than a favour from a
neighbour.

Furniture and fittings wear at the same accelerated rate. Exterior timber, upholstery, awnings and
shading structures on an exposed site are consumables on a Mykonos property rather than fixtures, and a
sensible budget replaces them on a cycle rather than when they fail.

Access and parking run on the same scarcity. Many older properties sit on lanes no vehicle reaches, and
in high season the parking near them is fully taken from morning to night, which matters as much to a
long-term tenant as to a summer guest.

Water is the other line to establish before purchase. Supply on the island leans on desalination and
delivered water, a pool and a garden are heavy consumers of both, and the cost of filling and running
them through a Mykonos summer is not comparable to a mainland equivalent.

Add those together and the gap between gross and net on this island is wider than the arithmetic in the
yield section suggests on its own. A property that models well on rent alone can model poorly once the
caretaker, the winterisation, the pool contract and one unbudgeted August emergency are counted.

For a Golden Visa buyer the point is sharper still, because the qualifying asset cannot take the
short-term income that normally pays for all of this. You are carrying an island cost base on a
twelve-month residential rent, and the [short-term rental
ban](/guides/greece-golden-visa-no-short-term-rental/) is what makes that combination unavoidable rather
than a choice. Ask any seller for the last two years of actual running costs, and treat a figure offered
without invoices as marketing.

## Who Mykonos suits

A buyer who wants the house, will use it, has capital that can sit through a multi-year hold, and treats
the residence permit as a consequence of a lifestyle purchase rather than its purpose. On those terms the
island is coherent, and the seasonal exit window is a scheduling matter rather than a risk.

It suits badly anyone underwriting income or needing a predictable exit. A buyer in that position at the
same budget should look at the Athens Riviera, where the tram and a year-round population produce a
tenant in February and a buyer in November — [Glyfada](/areas/glyfada-property-investment/) is the
closest comparison at the same tier — or accept the €400,000 tier elsewhere and hold the difference in
cash.

## Three scenarios, and which one the calendar allows

Mykonos rewards a buyer whose timetable is their own and punishes one whose is not. These are the three
profiles that actually arrive, set against the constraint that governs the island.

| Scenario | Budget | What works | What the seasonal exit does to it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lifestyle owner, permit secondary | €1,200,000+ | Buy the house you want, off the price ceiling so the 120 m² test clears | Irrelevant — a long hold absorbs the window |
| Permit-first buyer at the floor | €800,000 | Only away from seafront rates; at €7,500/m² the certified area fails | Painful — a forced sale in winter waits 6 to 18 months |
| Split-strategy investor | €1,400,000+ | Qualifying unit on a twelve-month lease, second non-qualifying unit under ΑΜΑ for summer letting | Manageable, because only one asset is permit-bound |

The middle row is where most disappointment happens. A buyer with exactly the threshold and a fixed
timetable is the worst-matched profile for this island, and the honest advice is to look at the Riviera
or the mainland instead of stretching.

### Risks worth pricing before an offer

- **Certified area shortfall** — the single most common failure, and it is settled by an engineer's
  certificate rather than by negotiation.
- **A winter listing** — six to eighteen months of carrying costs while the market is absent.
- **Out-of-plan title** — buildability, legal road access and the aigialos boundary, none of which is
  visible in a photograph.
- **Water and waste** — seasonal supply limits and private treatment systems are running costs, not
  amenities.
