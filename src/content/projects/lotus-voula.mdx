## The price test is already passed, and that is the problem

On almost every Golden Visa purchase in Attica the binding constraint is €800,000. At Lotus it is not.
Public listing signals for the scheme run from roughly €850,000 to €1.85 million, and the entry of that
band already sits €50,000 above the statutory floor. A buyer here does not have to negotiate a deed
upward, worry about a unit slipping under the threshold, or read a price list looking for the one stack
that qualifies. The price test clears itself on the first line of every quote.

That inverts where the risk sits. When the money test is automatic, the only thing standing between a
buyer and a residence permit is the engineer's certificate, and on the Athens Riviera that certificate
counts the least valuable part of what is being sold.

## What the engineer counts, and what you are actually paying for

The 120 square metres the law asks for is *main usable area*, κύρια χρήση, as recorded on the Electronic
Building Identity. It is the enclosed interior. It is not the veranda, not the roof terrace, not the
storage room, not the parking space, and not the void a double-height living room borrows from the floor
above.

Now consider what a Riviera apartment is sold on. Voula's premium over inland Attica is bought with sea
aspect, floor level, outdoor living and the depth of a terrace you can put a table on. A ground-floor
unit with a garden and a top-floor unit with a wrap-around terrace can be marketed at very different
prices with almost identical enclosed footprints. The brochure figure that justifies the money is the one
the certificate is designed to exclude.

The failure mode is specific and it is not exotic. A buyer signs on a unit described as "165 m²", pays a
deposit, and discovers at certification that 48 of those metres are veranda. The enclosed area is 117,
the file fails on size, and the money is already committed under a preliminary agreement that says
nothing about square metres. Nothing about that sequence is unusual on the Riviera; it is what happens
when the marketing unit of account and the statutory unit of account are different quantities and nobody
insisted on the second one in writing.

So the single instruction for this scheme is narrow: get the enclosed area in writing, from a licensed
engineer, referenced to the Electronic Building Identity, before any non-refundable payment. Not the
architect's plan, not the brochure, not the developer's own summary.

## Voula is a different municipality from Glyfada, and it matters more than the postcode

Voula sits in the municipality of Vari-Voula-Vouliagmeni, not in Glyfada. For a Golden Visa file that
makes no difference at all: both are Attica, both are the €800,000 tier, and the regional unit on the
cadastral extract governs regardless. For everything after the permit it makes a good deal of
difference.

Vari-Voula-Vouliagmeni is a smaller, more residential municipality with less commercial frontage than
Glyfada and a supply pipeline constrained by how little developable coastal land remains inside it. That
is why the two addresses do not track each other on price, and why Voula stock tends to be bought by
end-users rather than by investors chasing yield. The practical consequence for a Lotus buyer is that
the resale pool is people who want to live there, which is a narrower and slower market than Glyfada's
and a much narrower one than central Athens.

None of that is a reason to avoid the scheme. It is a reason to stop modelling the exit as though
Riviera new-build were liquid.

## Reading the €850,000 to €1.85 million spread

A band that wide inside one boutique scheme is not a pricing error. It is telling you the units are not
variations on a theme: they are different products stacked in one building.

| What the band position usually signals | What to verify first |
|---|---|
| Around €850,000 to €1,100,000 | Enclosed area, because this is where a unit is most likely to sit near the 120 m² line |
| Around €1,100,000 to €1,450,000 | The aspect actually sold, which rooms face the sea, and from which floor |
| Around €1,450,000 to €1,850,000 | The finish schedule annexed to the contract, since the premium at this level is specification |

The buyer who is only after the permit should be looking at the bottom of that band and should assume
nothing about it. The buyer who wants the apartment should be looking further up and should stop
treating the Golden Visa as the reason for the purchase, because at €1.5 million the permit is a
by-product of a decision that was made on other grounds.

## Stage payments are riskier here precisely because the sums are larger

Greek off-plan practice does not automatically protect a buyer's instalments. There is no statutory
deposit-protection scheme that behaves like a UK-style warranty or a French VEFA guarantee, so what
protects the money is whatever the preliminary agreement says and nothing else.

At an €800,000 purchase a typical staged structure leaves a substantial balance with the developer
before any ownership transfers. At €1.5 million the same percentages leave proportionally more. The
exposure is not a fixed sum, it scales with the price, and Lotus sits at the upper end of the range this
site covers.

Three clauses do the work, and all three are negotiated before signing rather than after:

- **A bank guarantee or an escrow arrangement** covering instalments paid before the deed. Ask for it
  explicitly; it will not be offered.
- **A delay penalty with a date attached.** "During 2026" is not a date. A contractual longstop with a
  financial consequence is.
- **An annexed specification schedule.** The finish level is a large part of what the upper band is
  buying, and a render is not a schedule.

## What to put to Adonis Group before the preliminary agreement

Adonis Group markets from Alimos and sells actively into international Golden Visa channels. That is a
description of a sales operation, not a judgement about the build. The questions below are the ones that
convert marketing into something checkable, and they are worth asking in writing.

1. The building permit number, and confirmation that the issued permit matches the plans being sold.
2. The land registry position on the site: who owns it, through which entity, and what is registered
   against it.
3. Enclosed main usable area per unit, certified, with balconies and terraces itemised separately.
4. Whether the sale is subject to 24% VAT as a first delivery or to 3.09% transfer tax, confirmed by
   your own lawyer rather than by the seller, because the two produce very different closing costs on a
   deed of this size.
5. The stage-payment schedule with the security attached to each stage.

## Where this scheme sits against the alternative

Lotus is the mirror image of the other Attica off-plan scheme reviewed on this site. At
[Argyroupoli](/projects/argyroupoli/) the published top unit is 126.99 m² at €780,000: it plausibly
passes the size test and fails the price test by €20,000, so the only route to a permit is negotiating
the deed *upward*. At Lotus the price test cannot fail and the size test is the whole file.

That is a genuine choice rather than a ranking. A buyer with exactly €800,000 to deploy has a problem at
Lotus, because the entry is above that and the Riviera premium buys nothing the permit recognises. A
buyer with €1.2 million who wants to use the apartment has a problem at Argyroupoli, because the scheme
does not go there.

Long-term letting is the only income either can carry while it supports a permit. Twelve-month
residential leases are permitted; short-term tourist letting of the qualifying asset is prohibited
nationwide for the life of the permit, and on Riviera stock at this price the long-let yield is thin
enough that most owners here are not buying for income in the first place. The
[Voula area guide](/areas/voula-property-investment/) sets out the local rental picture, and the
[120 m² rule](/guides/greece-golden-visa-120-square-meter-rule/) explains exactly what the certificate
does and does not count.
