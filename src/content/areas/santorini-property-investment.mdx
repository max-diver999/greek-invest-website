**Quick answer:** Santorini is an €800,000 Golden Visa market where the price test is trivially met and
the 120 m² certified-area test is where files fail. At caldera-front rates of roughly €8,000 per square
metre the threshold buys about 100 square metres — twenty short of the statutory minimum — while the
same €800,000 clears the floor with room to spare in the island's inland and non-caldera villages. The
caldera premium and the residence permit are, at this budget, mutually exclusive.

That is the whole page in one line, and it is worth stating first because almost every other
consideration on Santorini is downstream of it.

---

## The premium you are paying for is the one the certificate refuses to count

Santorini's price structure is unusual even by Greek island standards: value is concentrated along a few
kilometres of caldera rim and falls away sharply behind it. A property's worth here is largely a function
of what it looks at.

The Golden Visa is indifferent to all of it. The statute asks for 120 square metres of main usable area,
κύρια χρήση, recorded on the Electronic Building Identity — enclosed interior, with terraces, external
stairs, storage and outdoor living excluded. On caldera stock those excluded elements are where the money
went.

| Position | Indicative €/m² | What €800,000 buys | 120 m² test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Caldera front, Oia and Imerovigli | ~€8,000 | ~100 m² | Fails |
| Caldera-adjacent, set back | €5,000 to €6,500 | ~123 to 160 m² | Marginal to comfortable |
| Fira and the central spine | €4,000 to €5,000 | ~160 to 200 m² | Comfortable |
| Inland villages and the east coast | €2,500 to €3,500 | ~228 to 320 m² | Comfortable |

The table is the decision. A buyer who wants Oia and a permit at €800,000 is asking for two things the
market cannot supply together; a buyer who will accept Pyrgos or Emporio gets both and change. The
[120 m² rule](/guides/greece-golden-visa-120-square-meter-rule/) sets out precisely which parts of a
Cycladic house the engineer counts.

## Cave houses are measured like any other building, and that is the point

Santorini's yposkafa — houses cut back into the caldera pumice — are the island's signature product and
they attract a particular confusion: that because they are carved rather than built, their area is
somehow indeterminate.

It is not. A yposkafo has a built façade with door and windows on the caldera side, its interior is
enclosed, and a Greek engineer measures it conventionally against the Electronic Building Identity. What
makes cave stock difficult for a Golden Visa file is not measurement ambiguity but arithmetic: these are
small, expensive, deeply desirable interiors, and small interiors at €8,000 a square metre reach €800,000
long before they reach 120 square metres.

Two further characteristics matter at purchase and neither is about the permit. Vaulted and irregular
ceiling heights affect how a space is used but not how it is certified. And excavation into pumice is
tightly controlled, so extending a cave house to reach a threshold is not a plan a buyer should assume is
available.

## What the island's water and season do to a holding cost

Santorini has no meaningful natural fresh water supply and runs on desalination and imported supply. For
an owner that is a running-cost line rather than a headline, and it sits alongside the other costs of
holding property on an island that empties in winter: seasonal maintenance access, freight for every
repair, and higher insurance on exposed cliff positions.

None of that is a reason to avoid the island. It is a reason to underwrite the holding cost properly
rather than modelling a mainland cost base against island prices, particularly on a Golden Visa asset
where the hold will be measured in years rather than seasons.

## The income the permit allows is the income the island does not generate

Short-term tourist letting of the qualifying deed is prohibited nationwide for the life of the permit.
Santorini's rental economy is almost entirely short-term, which leaves the twelve-month residential lease
as the only compliant route — and long-let gross yields here run 2.5% to 4.0%, because caldera purchase
prices reflect a global view market while long-term rents reflect what people who work on Santorini can
pay.

Buyers who want both the permit and seasonal income hold two properties: one qualifying asset on a
twelve-month lease, and a separate non-qualifying property registered under ΑΜΑ for licensed short
letting. That is the only compliant structure, and it doubles the capital rather than splitting it.

## Due diligence items specific to a caldera purchase

- **Geotechnical stability.** Caldera-rim positions sit on pumice and past landslip is a documented risk
  in specific locations. A geotechnical opinion is not an optional extra here.
- **Traditional-settlement rules.** Oia, Fira, Imerovigli and Pyrgos sit under settlement protection
  controlling volumes, materials, colours and openings. What can be altered after purchase is narrower
  than a renovation budget usually assumes.
- **Ephorate oversight**, since works on a protected island stop when something is found.
- **Access and rights of way**, which on stepped caldera villages are frequently shared, informal and
  undocumented.
- **Whether the property is licensed for tourist use**, because a tourist-use encumbrance on the title
  conflicts directly with the letting restriction a Golden Visa imposes.

Run these with a Greek lawyer and a local engineer before any non-refundable payment; the
[due diligence guide](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/) covers the parts common to every Greek
purchase.

## Where Santorini fits against the alternatives

Against [Mykonos](/areas/mykonos-property-investment/) at the same tier, the difference is which
constraint binds. Mykonos is a liquidity problem — the resale market has a season and a winter listing
waits for spring. Santorini is a geometry problem — the buyer pool is year-round in the sense that
international demand for a caldera house never really stops, but the square metres at the price simply
are not there.

Against the €400,000 tier, the comparison is stark and worth making explicitly: the same €800,000 that
fails to qualify on the caldera would buy two qualifying properties elsewhere in Greece, or one with
substantial change. A buyer whose objective is residence rather than a Santorini house should read the
[Golden Visa property tiers](/guides/greece-golden-visa-property-tiers-2026/) before committing to this
island at all.

## A decision framework for the caldera premium

Every Santorini purchase resolves to one question asked in a specific order: what is the budget, and is
the view negotiable. Answer those two and the island sorts itself.

| If your budget is | And the view is | Then |
|---|---|---|
| €800,000 | Non-negotiable | You are buying a house, not a permit — the certified area will not reach 120 m² at €8,000/m² |
| €800,000 | Negotiable | Fira, the central spine or the inland villages clear the floor with 40 to 200 m² to spare |
| €1,200,000 and above | Non-negotiable | Both are achievable; verify the certificate rather than assuming it |
| Any | You want income | Reconsider the island — long-let gross of 2.5 to 4.0% reflects rents set by residents |

### Risks that are specific to this island

Geotechnical stability on the caldera rim is the one that cannot be fixed after purchase, and it belongs
in the file as a paid opinion rather than as an assumption. Traditional-settlement rules in Oia, Fira,
Imerovigli and Pyrgos control volumes, materials and openings, so the renovation a buyer is planning may
not be permitted. And a tourist-use encumbrance on the title is incompatible with the letting restriction
a Golden Visa imposes, which is a checklist item rather than a judgement call.
