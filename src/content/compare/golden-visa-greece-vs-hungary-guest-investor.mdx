**Quick answer:** Hungary's Guest Investor Programme is a subscription to a regulated real-estate fund at
around €250,000, with a long permit attached. Greece's Golden Visa is the purchase of a specific property
at €400,000 or €800,000 on a single deed. The headline gap in capital is real, and it is not the thing
that should decide the choice: what differs is what you own at the end of it.

---

## Fund units against a notary deed

The two programmes ask for money and give back status. What sits in between is entirely different.

| | Hungary Guest Investor | Greece Golden Visa |
|---|---|---|
| What you acquire | Units in a regulated real-estate fund | A specific property on one title deed |
| Indicative entry | Near €250,000 | €400,000 regional, €800,000 prime |
| Who chooses the asset | The fund manager | You |
| Can you occupy it | No | Yes |
| Can you let it | Not applicable — you hold units | Twelve-month residential leases only |
| Exit route | Redemption under the fund's rules | Sale of the property on the open market |
| What your heirs receive | Units, subject to fund terms | A Greek property, subject to Greek succession |

A buyer who reads only the first two rows concludes Hungary is Greece at a discount. A buyer who reads
the rest is choosing between a financial instrument and a building, and those are different asset classes
with different risks, not the same asset at two prices.

## The exit is where the difference becomes concrete

A Greek deed exits into a property market. It is slow, it is transaction-heavy, and it is yours to time:
you list when you choose, at a price you set, to whoever will pay it. Selling the qualifying asset ends
the basis for the permit unless it is replaced, which is a constraint on timing rather than on
marketability.

Fund units exit through redemption on the fund's terms. Those terms govern notice periods, lock-ins,
valuation methodology and any gating provision that applies when many investors redeem at once. None of
that is unusual for a fund and none of it is under your control. Read the fund documentation on
redemption before the subscription, because it is the only part of the Hungarian route that decides
whether your capital is accessible when you need it.

## What the Greek deed asks that a subscription does not

The Greek route carries obligations a fund subscription simply does not have, and they are the reason the
Greek file takes longer.

The property must sit in the correct tier for its cadastral parcel, certify 120 square metres of main
usable area on the Electronic Building Identity, sit on a single title, and be funded by a transfer
arriving from abroad through a Greek credit institution in the buyer's own name under Circular 1/2026.
Short-term tourist letting of it is prohibited for the life of the permit. Each of those is a place a
file can fail, and the [due diligence guide](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/) covers the checks.

A Hungarian subscription substitutes fund-level diligence for property-level diligence. You are not
verifying a cadastre entry; you are assessing a manager, a mandate and a redemption clause. Neither is
obviously easier — they are different competences, and a buyer should be honest about which one they
have.

## Use, family and the things a fund cannot do

The Greek property is a place. You can spend the summer in it, move a parent into it, or let it on a
twelve-month lease to a tenant. Greek family provisions extend the permit to a spouse, children under 21
and both sets of parents on the same single qualifying investment, which is unusually broad.

A fund unit does none of that. It produces no accommodation, houses nobody, and its family provisions are
whatever the programme's own rules say rather than a consequence of the asset. For a buyer whose motive
includes actually being somewhere, that gap is the decision.

## Why neither entry figure is the commitment

Comparing €250,000 against €400,000 as though they were the total commitment overstates the Hungarian
advantage in one direction and understates it in another, and the two sides go wrong for opposite
reasons.

The Greek figure understates because a deed sits under a stack of one-off acquisition charges and then a
building that has to be maintained, insured and taxed for as long as it is owned. All of it is
enumerated in the [cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-greece/), and the useful feature
of it is that every line arrives as a bill from somebody, so nothing hides.

The Hungarian figure understates for the opposite reason: nothing arrives as a bill at all. Subscription
charges, ongoing management and any performance participation are deducted inside the fund, so the
investor's experience is a value that rises less than the underlying assets did rather than a cost they
paid. Over a ten-year holding that difference is not cosmetic, and it is the single number the
programme's marketing is least likely to put in front of you.

The honest summary is that Hungary is cheaper to enter, Greece produces an asset you can occupy, and the
long-run totals are closer than the headline entry figures suggest — but only a buyer who has read the
fund's fee schedule and a Greek cost breakdown side by side is in a position to say by how much.

## Red flags in this comparison

- **Material that calls the Hungarian route a property investment.** It is a fund subscription; the fund
  holds property.
- **Any comparison that stops at the entry figure** without the ongoing fee and cost stack on both sides.
- **Assuming Hungarian units can be occupied or let.** They cannot.
- **Assuming a Greek deed can be redeemed quickly.** Property does not redeem; it sells.
- **Treating either programme as permanent.** Both are creatures of national policy, and Greece's own
  thresholds were rewritten in 2024.

## Buyer scenarios: what each route actually suits

**Lowest capital for an EU permit, no interest in the asset.** Hungary. Read the redemption terms first
and treat them as the product.

**A Mediterranean property you will use.** Greece, and the extra capital buys a building rather than a
better programme.

**Family across three generations on one investment.** Greece, because spouse, children under 21 and four
parents ride on the same qualifying purchase.

**Rental income alongside residence.** Neither, in one asset. Greek qualifying property is limited to
twelve-month lets and Hungarian units are not property at all; the structure that works is a qualifying
asset plus a separate non-qualifying one.

## What holding a fund unit for the term involves

The Greek side of the holding question is ordinary property ownership and is covered in the
[cost of buying guide](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-greece/). The Hungarian side is where buyers have
no intuition, so it is the half worth setting out.

A fund unit has a net asset value, not a price. It is calculated by the manager on the fund's own
valuation cycle, and between calculation dates there is no market quotation to check it against. An
apartment has an asking price you can compare with the flat downstairs; a unit has a number the manager
publishes. Neither is more honest than the other, but only one of them can be sanity-checked by an
outsider.

Management fees accrue against the fund rather than arriving as invoices. That is the structural
difference from a building, where every cost shows up as a bill somebody has to pay. Fund costs are
netted out of the value before you ever see it, which makes them easy to underestimate over a decade and
no smaller for being invisible. Ask for the total expense figure, not the headline management fee.

The fund's own concentration matters too. If the mandate holds a narrow set of Hungarian commercial
buildings, the unit is exposed to one city's office or retail market rather than to a diversified
portfolio, and that exposure is not disclosed on the programme's marketing material. It is in the fund
documentation, and reading it is the closest Hungarian equivalent to a Greek buyer's cadastral and
engineer's checks.

## Ten years against five, and what each renewal tests

The permit terms are not the same shape. The Hungarian guest investor permit is issued for a long fixed
term measured in years rather than the five-year cycle Greece runs, and Greece re-tests the same fact at
every renewal: do you still own the qualifying asset, on the terms the
[renewal requirements](/guides/greece-golden-visa-renewal-requirements/) set out. A longer term means fewer touchpoints with an
immigration authority, which buyers reasonably prefer, and it also means the programme's conditions are
locked in for longer on both sides of that bargain. Confirm the current term and renewal conditions with
Hungarian counsel, because this is the part of the programme most likely to have been amended since any
article you are reading was written.

## One of these is priced in euros and one is not

Hungary is in the European Union and outside the eurozone. Greece is in both. For a buyer whose money and
whose life are denominated in something else, that is not a technicality about which notes are in the
cash machine.

A Greek deed is a euro asset: the price, the rent, the costs and the eventual sale are all in the same
currency, and the only exchange risk is between the euro and the buyer's home currency. Hungarian fund
units add a second layer, because the fund's assets and its unit price sit in the Hungarian economy.
Whether that reaches you depends on the fund's own denomination and hedging policy, which is a question
for the fund documentation rather than for the programme's marketing, and it is worth asking directly:
in what currency is my subscription, in what currency is the net asset value calculated, and what happens
to my holding if the forint moves against it.

Neither answer is better in the abstract. Currency exposure is a risk you can be paid for as easily as
punished by. The point is that one of these two routes has it and the other does not, and a comparison
that stops at EUR 250,000 against EUR 400,000 has quietly assumed both figures behave the same way for the
next decade.

## Both are policy, and both have changed

Hungary is not new to investment migration. It ran a residency bond scheme in the 2010s, closed it, and
reopened a differently designed route years later under fresh legislation. Greece did not close its
programme but rewrote its thresholds in 2024, moving the entry price in Attica and the islands from a
figure that had stood for a decade.

The lesson from both histories is the same and it is the most useful thing on this page: neither route is
a right you acquire, and the terms you enter under are the terms as they stand on your application date,
not a guarantee for the life of the investment. A buyer who needs certainty about a decade from now is
choosing between two instruments that cannot supply it.
