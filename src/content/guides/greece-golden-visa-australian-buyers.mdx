**Quick answer:** Australia is the one buyer nationality on this site with **no comprehensive double
taxation agreement with Greece**. The two countries have an Airline Profits Agreement covering the
taxation of airline operations, and nothing that governs rental income, capital gains or residence. That
absence, rather than any Greek rule, is what makes an Australian purchase different.

**Read the Greek side elsewhere.** An Australian buys a Greek property the way everyone does, and those steps are documented separately: the [€400,000 and €800,000 tier map](/guides/greece-golden-visa-property-tiers-2026/), the [120 m² certified usable area test](/guides/greece-golden-visa-120-square-meter-rule/), the [short-term rental ban on the qualifying asset](/guides/greece-golden-visa-no-short-term-rental/), the [AFM and Greek bank account sequence](/guides/greece-golden-visa-bank-account-afm/), the [cadastre and engineer due diligence](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/). Everything below concerns what Australia does about it.

## What no treaty actually costs you

With a treaty, relief is a negotiated entitlement with defined rates and an arbitration mechanism.
Without one, relief is whatever each country's domestic law happens to give.

In practice Greek rental income is taxed in Greece first, as the source country, at rates from 15% to
45% charged on gross rent with only a flat 5% repairs allowance. The Australian Taxation Office then
assesses the same income as part of your assessable income, computed on Australian principles, and
relief comes through the **Foreign Income Tax Offset** rather than through a treaty article.

| With a comprehensive treaty | Australia and Greece, as things stand |
|---|---|
| Defined relief method and rates | Domestic FITO only |
| A residence tie-breaker article | None — dual residence has no treaty resolution |
| A mutual agreement procedure for disputes | None |
| Certainty on which state taxes what | Each country applies its own law |

The FITO is capped and, unlike some foreign credit systems, excess offset is not carried forward. Where
the Greek charge exceeds the Australian tax on the same income, the excess is simply lost.

## Dual residence with no tie-breaker is the risk worth naming

Spend more than 183 days in a Greek year and you can become a Greek tax resident on worldwide income.
Remain connected enough to Australia and you may still be an Australian resident for tax purposes at the
same time.

Between treaty countries, a tie-breaker article resolves exactly this: permanent home, then centre of
vital interests, then habitual abode, then nationality. Between Australia and Greece there is no such
article, so both countries can assess the same person on the same worldwide income under their own rules
and neither is obliged to yield.

That is a manageable position if it is planned and an expensive one if it is discovered. Any Australian
intending to spend substantial time in Greece should establish the day counts and the residency position
with advisers in both countries before, not after, the pattern is established.

## Superannuation is the pot that cannot buy this

Australians hold a large share of household wealth in superannuation, and it is the natural place to look
for the capital. It generally does not work.

A self-managed super fund is bound by the sole purpose test: the fund exists to provide retirement
benefits, and its assets must not provide a present-day benefit to members or their relatives. A Greek
apartment the family intends to visit fails that squarely. Related-party use, in-house asset limits, the
prohibition on acquiring certain assets from members, and the requirement that fund assets be held
separately from personal assets all point the same way.

There is a further and simpler problem. The Golden Visa requires the property to be held by the
applicant, and a fund is not the applicant. A structure that satisfies Australian superannuation law is
unlikely to satisfy the Greek permit conditions, and one that satisfies the permit is unlikely to be a
compliant fund investment.

The practical answer for most Australians is that this purchase is made with non-super capital. Anyone
determined to explore an SMSF route needs licensed Australian superannuation advice first and a Greek
lawyer second, in that order.

## Capital gains, and the suspension that does not reach you

Greek capital gains tax on property has been suspended, so a disposal during the suspension attracts no
Greek CGT. Australian CGT still applies to the gain on a property held by an Australian resident, and
with no Greek tax paid there is no foreign tax to offset against it.

The Australian discount for assets held more than twelve months may apply, and the cost base is
established in Australian dollars, so the AUD/EUR movement over the holding period forms part of the
gain whether or not the property itself moved in euro terms. Confirm the suspension's current end date
before modelling any disposal.

## The two tax years do not line up, and the offset has to be matched across them

Australia's income year runs 1 July to 30 June. Greece's runs on the calendar year. For an Australian
claiming the Foreign Income Tax Offset on Greek rental income, that six-month displacement is not a
formality — it is the thing that makes the claim awkward.

The offset is available for foreign tax paid on income that is also assessable in Australia. Establishing
that means matching a Greek assessment covering January to December against an Australian return covering
July to June, so a single Australian income year draws on two Greek tax years and neither Greek
assessment corresponds to it. Add the timing of when Greek tax is actually assessed and paid, which is
after the Greek year has closed, and the Australian return is frequently due before the Greek liability
for the overlapping period is final.

Three practical consequences. Keep Greek records on a monthly basis rather than an annual one, because
apportioning a Greek annual figure across Australian income years is far easier with monthly data than
by reconstruction afterwards. Expect to amend: where the Greek assessment lands after the Australian
return has been lodged, an amendment is the ordinary way through rather than a sign something went wrong.
And translate at the right rates, because both the income and the foreign tax convert to Australian
dollars, and the conventions for doing that are prescriptive rather than a matter of choice.

This compounds the offset problem the page has already described. The Foreign Income Tax Offset is capped
and its excess is not carried forward, so where the Greek charge exceeds the Australian tax on the same
income the surplus is simply lost. A mismatch that pushes Greek tax and Australian income into different
years can create exactly that outcome artificially: a year with Greek tax and little matched Australian
income, and another with the reverse. With no comprehensive treaty and therefore no mutual agreement
procedure, there is no mechanism to ask the two revenue authorities to resolve it between them.

Withholding is the detail that catches people at the Greek end of the same problem. Tax deducted in
Greece and tax finally assessed there are not always the same figure, and it is the final Greek liability
rather than the amount withheld that supports the offset.

None of this is a reason not to buy. It is a reason to have an Australian accountant who has done a
foreign rental property before, engaged before the first tenant rather than at the first return.

## Advantages and disadvantages for an Australian buyer

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Residence-based taxation, so relocation genuinely changes the position | No comprehensive treaty, so no defined relief and no dispute mechanism |
| The FITO gives real relief where Greek tax is lower than Australian | Excess FITO is not carried forward and is simply lost |
| Greek property is an ordinary asset with no Australian pre-approval needed | No residence tie-breaker, so dual residence has no resolution |
| The permit carries no minimum stay | Superannuation, where much Australian capital sits, cannot fund it |

## Risks an Australian buyer should price before signing

- **Assuming treaty relief exists.** It does not; check the current position, because negotiations
  toward a comprehensive agreement would change this page materially.
- **Drifting into dual residence** through day counts nobody was tracking.
- **Losing excess FITO** where the Greek charge exceeds the Australian tax on the same income.
- **Attempting an SMSF purchase** without licensed superannuation advice.
- **Ignoring currency in the cost base**, since the gain is computed in Australian dollars.

## Buyer scenarios where the Australian position bites

**The Melbourne or Sydney buyer holding non-super capital.** The cleanest case. Expect Greek tax on the
rent, an Australian assessment on the same income, and FITO relief that is imperfect at the top of the
Greek scale.

**The buyer planning long Greek summers.** Track the days from the first year. Without a tie-breaker
article, a residence question resolved badly is resolved twice.

**The buyer whose wealth is in super.** Treat this purchase as requiring capital outside the fund, and
take Australian advice before assuming otherwise.

## The cost base is assembled in Australian dollars, one payment at a time

None of the euro figures in a Greek purchase is the number the ATO will use. Your cost base is denominated
in Australian dollars, assembled from the AUD equivalent of each payment on the day it settled — the
reservation, the deposit, the completion balance, the taxes, the professional fees. A purchase spanning six
months and three exchange rates produces a cost base that exists in no single document.

Two consequences follow. Record the rate, the date and the AUD amount for every outbound payment as it
happens, because reconstructing them years later from bank archives is expensive and sometimes impossible.
And understand that currency movement is part of your gain: a property flat in euro terms can produce a
taxable Australian gain purely because the dollar weakened over the holding period, and the twelve-month
CGT discount, where it applies, applies to that combined figure rather than to the euro movement alone.

## Which Australian residency test catches you, and when

Because there is no treaty tie-breaker, the only thing standing between you and dual taxation is the
Australian residency analysis itself, and it is not a single day count.

An individual is an Australian resident if they satisfy any one of several tests: ordinary residence
judged on the facts, domicile unless a permanent place of abode has been established elsewhere, the
183-day test in an income year, or the Commonwealth superannuation test. Failing to spend 183 days in
Australia does not by itself end residency, because domicile and ordinary-residence reasoning can keep you
in the system while you are abroad.

| What buyers assume | What actually applies |
|---|---|
| Leaving Australia for a year ends residency | Domicile persists until a permanent place of abode is established elsewhere |
| Under 183 days means non-resident | The 183-day test is one route in, not the only route |
| Owning a Greek home helps prove departure | Ties in Australia — property, family, employment, accounts — weigh against it |
| A tax adviser can resolve a clash later | Without a treaty there is no mutual agreement procedure to resolve it |

The practical instruction is unglamorous: if you expect to spend long periods in Greece, get an Australian
residency position in writing before the pattern establishes itself rather than after two years of it.

## What a comprehensive treaty would change, if one is concluded

Australia and Greece have periodically discussed a comprehensive agreement. If one is concluded, four
things change at once for Australian owners: defined relief in place of a domestic offset, capped treaty
rates on some income, a residence tie-breaker article, and a mutual agreement procedure for disputes.

That is not a reason to delay a purchase, but it is a reason to check the position rather than inherit it
from an article written earlier. Any Australian relying on this page should confirm the current treaty
status before making decisions that assume its absence.
