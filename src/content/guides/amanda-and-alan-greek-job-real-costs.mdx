**Quick answer:** the series was filmed on Corfu and went out on BBC One from
2 January 2026, starting from a property acquired for a nominal €1 and later listed
at €530,000 with the proceeds going to Comic Relief and BBC Children in Need. The €1
is a production and charity arrangement rather than a market route, and Greece has no
one-euro house scheme. A real Corfu purchase runs roughly €2,932 to €4,698 per square
metre in asking prices depending on where on the island you look, plus 7 to 10 percent
in acquisition costs, plus a renovation budget whose real risk is what gets found
rather than what gets quoted.

A renovation series is a good programme and a poor budget. That is not a criticism:
the format exists to compress a long, uneven, administratively dull process into an
hour that people want to watch, and it does that well. But a large number of people
finish an episode and start searching for houses in Greece, and the gap between the
edit and the process is worth setting out for them.

This page is that gap, with the numbers that can be sourced and honest silence where
they cannot.

## What the programme actually did

| Element | What it was |
|---|---|
| Broadcaster and dates | BBC One and iPlayer, from 2 January 2026, filmed the preceding summer |
| Location | Corfu, in the Ionian islands |
| Property | A two-bedroom, one-bathroom house, named Alamanda for the series |
| Acquisition price | A nominal €1 |
| Listed afterwards | €530,000, with proceeds to Comic Relief and BBC Children in Need |

Everything in that table is a fact about a television production. None of it is a
description of the Greek property market, and the programme has never claimed
otherwise. The confusion arrives afterwards, in the search results.

## The €1 house, and why it is not a route

Greece does not run a one-euro house scheme. Italy has done so in depopulating
villages, with conditions attached about renovation deadlines and deposits, and the
idea has travelled further than the geography. In Greece the nominal price in the
series is an arrangement specific to a production and a charity sale.

What does exist in Greece is genuinely cheap stock in remote places. Inland areas of
the Ionian islands carry asking prices around €1,334 per square metre at the bottom
of the range, and abandoned or part-ruined village houses come up regularly for
figures that look startling to a British or American buyer.

Those are ordinary sales. They are cheap because they are far from services, often
without adopted road access or mains drainage, frequently held in undivided family
ownership across several heirs, and almost always carrying decades of unpermitted
alteration. The purchase price is the smallest number in the project.

For wider context, the Aegean Islands are the most expensive Greek region at about

5.7 percent year on year in the first quarter of 2026, fastest at 6.9 percent outside

Corfu prices at this level because it has carried an international buying market for
decades, with the airlift and the foreign-owned stock that follow. The figures are
asking prices from live listings, meaning opening positions rather than recorded

For a cheaper Ionian alternative with the same landscape and a thinner resale market,
[Kefalonia](/property-for-sale/kefalonia/) is the usual comparison.

## Renovation cost is a discovery problem

The question everyone asks is what a renovation costs per square metre. It is the
wrong question, and any figure offered for an unseen property is a guess dressed as
expertise.

What can be described honestly is the structure of the cost.

| Cost element | What drives it |
|---|---|
| Engineer's inspection and legality certificate | Age and history of the building, not its size |
| Permits | Whether works are structural or change the footprint |
| Materials | Island transport premium on everything that arrives by ferry |
| Labour | Availability far more than hourly rate |
| Contingency | What is found once a wall comes down |

The last row is the one that decides budgets. Older Greek stone stock hides
substandard wiring, absent damp proofing, roofs that were repaired rather than
replaced, and additions built without a permit by a previous owner. None of that is
visible on a viewing and all of it is chargeable.

The [engineer certificate guide](/guides/greece-property-engineer-certificate/)
covers what the inspection actually certifies and why it is the document that decides
whether a sale can complete at all.

## The constraint nobody films

Contractor availability on a Greek island is seasonal, and it is the single largest
difference between a television renovation and a private one.

A production books its crew in advance and works to a broadcast date. A private owner
is competing for a small pool of local builders, electricians and plumbers whose
numbers thin out sharply between November and March, in exactly the months when a
buyer would prefer the work to happen so the house is ready for summer. Materials
that have to come by ferry arrive on a winter timetable. Specialist trades may not be
on the island at all.

The practical planning consequence is that an island renovation timeline is set by
when people are available rather than by the size of the job, and that a schedule
built from mainland assumptions will slip.

## What it costs before any renovation at all

Acquisition costs run 7 to 10 percent above the purchase price, and they are payable
whatever condition the house is in.

On a resale the largest item is the property transfer tax at an effective 3.09 percent
of the taxable value, being the 3 percent statutory rate plus the municipal surcharge.
The base is the higher of the state's objective value and the declared price, which is
why the objective value belongs in the arithmetic before a deposit rather than at the
notary. A first sale by a developer falls under VAT at 24 percent instead, subject to
the suspension regime.

Notary, lawyer, land registry and agent make up the rest, and Greek VAT at 24 percent
applies on top of each professional fee, so a quoted fee is not the amount that leaves
the account. The [full cost stack](/guides/hidden-costs-buying-property-greece/)
itemises it and the
[transfer tax guide](/guides/greece-property-transfer-tax-fma/) explains the objective
value.

Worked on the sort of figure a viewer has in mind, a €120,000 village house carries
about €3,708 in transfer tax at the 3.09 percent rate and an all-in acquisition stack
of roughly €8,400 to €12,000. A €250,000 house carries about €7,725 in transfer tax
and a stack of roughly €17,500 to €25,000. Neither number includes a single day of
building work, and neither is negotiable.

## What to check before falling for a ruin

Four things, and each has a document that closes it. The
[full due diligence sequence](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/) covers the rest.

**Building legality.** Extensions, enclosed terraces and converted outbuildings built
without permits are extremely common on older island stock. The engineer's legality
certificate will not cover them, and without it the notary cannot complete. This is
the most frequent reason a cheap house turns out not to be buyable.

**The title chain.** Village properties frequently sit in undivided inheritance across
several heirs, some abroad. Every one of them signs, and the one who is hardest to
reach sets the timetable.

**Access and services.** Whether the road is adopted, where the water comes from, and
whether mains drainage reaches the plot. Cheap inland plots are often cheap for
precisely these reasons, and connecting them can cost more than the house did.

**What the survey cannot see.** A pre-purchase inspection reduces discovery risk; it
does not eliminate it. Budget a contingency you would be comfortable spending, because
on a renovation of older stock you probably will.

## Does renovating beat buying finished?

The honest answer is that it depends on a number most people do not calculate before
they start: the discount for condition against the cost of curing it.

Corfu asking prices run about €2,932 per square metre in the cheapest municipality of
the prefecture and about €4,698 in Kassiopi. A 120 square metre finished house in the
cheaper municipality therefore asks somewhere near €352,000 before costs, and the same
size in Kassiopi near €564,000. Add 7 to 10 percent in acquisition costs, so on a
€352,000 purchase the transfer tax alone at 3.09 percent is about €10,877 and the full
stack lands roughly between €24,600 and €35,200.

A comparable property needing full renovation trades at a discount to those figures.
Whether the project makes sense turns on whether that discount exceeds the renovation
cost plus the contingency plus the value of the eighteen months you did not have the
house. On older Greek island stock the discount is frequently large enough that the
answer is yes, which is why the market for renovation projects exists at all.

Two further numbers belong in the comparison. Around 78 percent of international
buyers in Greece target resale rather than new build, so the renovation route is the
mainstream one here rather than the adventurous one. And Bank of Greece data has
apartment prices up 5.7 percent year on year in the first quarter of 2026, with areas
outside the main cities the fastest segment at 6.9 percent, which means the finished
comparable is a moving target while the works are under way.

## If the plan is to let it afterwards

Many viewers are picturing a house that pays for itself between visits, so the letting
arithmetic belongs in the budget rather than after it.

Greek rental income for an individual landlord runs on a scale that changed on
1 January 2026.

| Annual gross rent | Rate | Gross yield benchmark | |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Up to €12,000 | 15% | Athens, city-wide | 5.43% |
| €12,001 to €24,000 | 25% | Greece, national | 4.40% |
| €24,001 to €35,000 | 35% | Kavala, weakest major city | 3.47% |
| Above €35,000 | 45% | Deduction allowed for repairs | 5% |

Only that flat 5% deduction for repairs and maintenance may be set against gross rent,
with nothing allowed for management, insurance or finance costs, so the charge falls
close to the gross figure rather than on profit. Island yields look stronger on a peak
nightly rate and weaker once the empty months are averaged in.

One restriction overrides all of it. If the purchase is also a Golden Visa file, the
property may only be let on leases of twelve months or longer, and short-term tourist
letting is prohibited for the life of the permit. The application fees are €2,000 for
the main applicant and €150 for each adult family member. A renovated island house
cannot be both a Golden Visa asset and a summer let.

## Buyer scenarios

**A UK couple buying a €180,000 village house on Corfu to renovate over two years.**
Acquisition costs of 7 to 10 percent add roughly €12,600 to €18,000, with transfer tax
at 3.09 percent about €5,562. The controlling risks are building legality on a house
that has probably been altered without a permit, and an inheritance chain in a village
property. The controlling constraint is trade availability between November and March.
Not a Golden Visa file at this price, so letting stays unrestricted.

**A buyer taking a €400,000 finished house on Corfu as a Golden Visa file.** The
€400,000 tier applies outside the prime zone, the property must be a single dwelling
on one title deed with at least 120 square metres of main usable area certified by an
engineer, and transfer tax at 3.09 percent is €12,360. The renovation question does
not arise, and the letting question does: a qualifying property may only be let on
leases of twelve months or longer.

**A buyer weighing a €250,000 restoration route.** The reduced Golden Visa threshold
applies to conversion from another use or restoration of a listed building, and it is
the one tier exempt from the 120 square metre rule. It is also a construction project
with a completion obligation running alongside the permit application, which suits
people who have done this kind of work before and rarely suits a first overseas
purchase. Watching a renovation series is not the same as having done one.

## The realistic version of the programme

Strip out the production and the timeline compresses differently. A private buyer
doing the equivalent project on Corfu is looking at a real purchase price rather than
a nominal one, 7 to 10 percent on top in acquisition costs, a renovation budget whose
range widens the older the building is, and a schedule set by island trade
availability rather than by a broadcast date.

None of that argues against doing it. Plenty of people buy and renovate on the Ionian
islands successfully every year, and the result is frequently better value than an
equivalent finished property, because the discount for condition in Greece is often
larger than the cost of curing it.

What it argues for is starting from the engineer and the lawyer rather than from the
estate agent's photographs, and treating the programme as the reason you looked rather
than as the model for how it goes.
