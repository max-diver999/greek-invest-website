**Quick answer:** the cheapest property in Greece is on the northern mainland, in
towns like Kastoria at around €530 per square metre, Kozani around €640, Kilkis
around €680 and Grevena around €820. The cheapest island areas run two to four times
that, with inland Kefalonia near €1,334 and inland Zakynthos near €1,997. Greece has
no one-euro house scheme. Cheap stock is cheap because of distance from services,
population decline, condition and legal complication, and each of those is
discoverable before a deposit moves.

Almost everyone searching for cheap property in Greece is picturing an island. That
is the single most expensive assumption in this part of the market, and correcting it
is what this page is for.

## Where cheap actually is

| Where | Asking price per m² | Kind of place |
|---|---:|---|
| Kastoria | about €530 | Northern mainland town, lakeside |
| Kozani | about €640 | Northern mainland town |
| Kilkis | about €680 | Northern mainland town |
| Grevena | about €820 | Northern mainland town |
| Kefalonia, Omala | about €1,334 | Cheapest end of an Ionian island |
| Zakynthos, Artemisio | about €1,997 | Cheapest end of a busier Ionian island |

Those mainland figures come from published regional market summaries rather than from
a primary index, and the island figures from Indomio, a Greek property portal. Both
are asking prices from listings rather than recorded transactions, which the
[market overview](/property-for-sale/) explains at length.

The gap is the point. Kastoria is roughly two and a half times cheaper per square
metre than the cheapest part of Kefalonia and close to four times cheaper than the
cheapest part of Zakynthos. Nobody browsing Greek property listings from abroad ends
up in Kastoria by accident, which is precisely why the price is what it is.

The broader pattern: the Peloponnese, Epirus in the northwest and Thrace in the
northeast carry the lowest regional prices in the country, and northern Greece
generally sits well below the national picture.

## What a budget actually buys

Turning a price per square metre into floor area makes the range concrete.

| Budget | At Kastoria, about €530/m² | At Grevena, about €820/m² | At inland Kefalonia, about €1,334/m² |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| €50,000 | about 94 m² | about 61 m² | about 37 m² |
| €100,000 | about 188 m² | about 122 m² | about 75 m² |
| €150,000 | about 283 m² | about 183 m² | about 112 m² |

Two adjustments belong on top of every cell. Acquisition costs come off buying power
before anything else, and the
[cost stack guide](/guides/hidden-costs-buying-property-greece/) sets out what they
consist of. And condition is the variable a per-square-metre average cannot capture:
at this end of the market the difference between a habitable house and a shell is
frequently larger than the purchase price itself.

## The one-euro house question

Greece has no one-euro house scheme. Italy has run such programmes in depopulating
villages, with conditions on renovation deadlines and deposits attached, and the idea
has travelled further than the geography.

What Greece does have is genuinely cheap village stock, particularly inland and in
the north, some of it abandoned or part-ruined, sold as ordinary transactions at
ordinary prices. Occasionally those prices look startling from abroad. They are still
sales, with a title chain, a cadastre entry and an engineer's certificate to obtain.

The reliable rule at this end of the market is that the purchase price is the smallest
number in the project. A house bought at €30,000 needing full renovation is a
renovation budget with a small acquisition attached, not a bargain house.

## What the cheap places are actually like

Naming towns without describing them is how listings mislead, so it is worth saying
what these places are before anyone treats a price per square metre as an
opportunity.

**Kastoria** sits on a lake in the northwest, ringed by mountains, historically a fur
trading town with a stock of stone and timber houses that is genuinely distinctive.
It has a small airport with domestic connections, cold winters and real snow, and a
population that has been shrinking for years. It is a place people live rather than a
place people visit, and that is exactly why the price per square metre is what it is.

**Kozani and Grevena** are northern mainland towns with the same basic profile:
functioning, unremarkable, well below the national price level, and a long way from
anywhere an international buyer would arrive by default. Both sit near mountain
country that gets serious winters and, in a couple of places, ski infrastructure.

**Kilkis** is close to the northern border and to Thessaloniki, which makes it the
one on this list with a plausible commuting relationship to a real city.

The common thread is that none of these places is trying to sell itself to a foreign
buyer, and none of them has a market that depends on one. That has two consequences.
Prices are set by local demand rather than by what an overseas buyer will pay, which
is the good half. And the resale pool is local too, which is the other half.

## Running costs at the budget end

One genuine advantage of buying cheaply in Greece rarely gets mentioned: the annual
costs scale with the property, and at this end they are small.

ENFIA, the annual property tax, is assessed on the objective value the state assigns
rather than on what you paid, and in low-value areas that assessment is correspondingly
low. Utilities, insurance and maintenance follow the size and condition of the
building. A modest mainland house is inexpensive to hold in a way that a coastal villa
is not.

That matters for the calculation people actually face, which is not "will this
appreciate" but "can I afford to keep this while I use it a few months a year". At
Kastoria or Grevena prices, the answer is usually yes by a comfortable margin, and it
stays yes even in a year when nothing is let and nothing is sold.

The exception is a property under renovation, where holding costs and works run
concurrently and the budget has to carry both. That is the situation where cheap
purchases most often become uncomfortable, and it is a reason to buy something
habitable first if the finances are tight.

## Why cheap property is cheap

Four reasons recur, and none of them is hidden.

**Distance.** From an airport, a hospital, employment and a supermarket. Every one of
those raises the cost of living there and lowers the pool of people who want to.

**Population decline.** Much of the cheapest Greek stock is in regions losing
population. That is why supply exceeds demand, and it is also why resale is slow.

**Condition.** A large share of very cheap stock has not been meaningfully maintained
for decades. Wiring, damp, roofing and plumbing are the usual four, and none is
visible on a viewing.

**Legal complication.** Undivided inheritances where six heirs each hold a share.
Extensions and enclosed terraces built without permits, which the engineer cannot
certify. Plots without adopted road access or mains water and drainage. These are the
reasons a specific cheap house is cheap even for its area.

The first two are strategic and you accept them or you do not. The second two are
checkable, and the [due diligence sequence](/guides/due-diligence-greece-property/)
sets out how, with the
[engineer certificate](/guides/greece-property-engineer-certificate/) and the
[cadastre check](/guides/greece-property-cadastre-check/) doing most of the work.

## A good purchase is not the same as a good investment

This is the distinction the budget end of any market blurs, and it is worth stating
plainly.

Buying cheaply in a depopulating region for lifestyle reasons is frequently a sound
decision. The house is affordable to own, the running costs are low, and the money at
risk is small.

Buying cheaply there for yield or capital growth usually is not. Thin markets are
thin in both directions: the same shortage of buyers that produced the low entry price
is waiting when you sell. Rental demand outside tourist areas and cities is limited,
and a property that takes two years to sell has costs the spreadsheet did not model.

If the purpose is investment rather than a home, the market where the buyers are is a
different market from the one where the prices are low. The
[market overview](/property-for-sale/) sets out where yields and liquidity actually
sit.

## Risks and red flags at the budget end

**A price that is low even for its own area.** The mainland figures above are area
averages, so a house asking well under them is asking under an already low number.
That is sometimes condition and sometimes a title problem, and the difference is
worth establishing before anything else, because one is a budget question and the
other is a whether-you-can-buy-it question.

**Listings that are not really for sale.** Very cheap advertised properties sometimes
turn out to be a share of an undivided inheritance, or a property whose owner has not
in fact agreed to sell. Ask early who is instructing the agent.

**Unpermitted building work.** The most common reason a cheap purchase cannot
complete, because the engineer cannot issue the legality certificate the notary needs.

**No adopted access or services.** Connecting a plot to mains water, drainage or
power can cost more than the house did. Establish the position before an offer.

**Renovation quotes that assume mainland logistics.** In remote areas and on islands,
materials and trades cost more and arrive slower, and the quote from a contractor two
hours away is not the quote you will pay.

**Pressure to move fast on an unusually low price.** The
[property scams guide](/guides/greece-property-scams-avoid/) covers the patterns worth
recognising, and urgency is the common thread in most of them.

**Buying without seeing it in winter.** At this end of the market the winter version
of a village is materially quieter than the summer one, and it is the version you
would live in. In the northern towns that difference includes real cold and snow,
which is not what most people picture when they picture Greece and which changes both
the heating bill and the character of the place.

## Buyer scenarios

**A buyer with €60,000 wanting a habitable house.** The northern mainland, where the
budget corresponds to roughly 113 square metres at Kastoria prices. Realistic if the
house is sound. The controlling checks are title chain, building legality and services.
Not a rental proposition and not intended as one.

**A buyer with €100,000 wanting an island.** Achievable inland on Kefalonia at about
75 square metres of built area, with the trade being distance from the coast and
services. On Zakynthos the same money buys about 50 square metres at the island's
cheapest end. Anyone expecting a coastal island house at this budget is looking at
the wrong price bracket.

**A buyer with €150,000 planning a renovation.** The purchase is the smaller half.
Budget the works with a contingency you would be comfortable spending, get the
engineer in before the deposit rather than after, and price contractor availability
rather than contractor rates. The
[resale checklist](/guides/resale-property-greece-checklist-foreigner/) covers what to
verify on existing stock.
