**Quick answer:** Canada taxes on residence rather than citizenship, so a Canadian who genuinely ceases
to be a Canadian tax resident stops filing on worldwide income. While resident, the item that catches
Canadians buying in Greece is not a tax charge at all — it is a disclosure form, T1135, and it is
triggered by cost rather than by income.

## T1135 is the form Canadians most often miss

If the total **cost amount** of your specified foreign property exceeds CAD 100,000 at any point in the
year, you must file Form T1135 with your return. A Greek apartment bought at either Golden Visa
threshold clears that line several times over on the day the deed is signed.

The form is a disclosure, not a tax. Filing it costs nothing beyond the accountant's time. Not filing it
carries penalties that accrue per month, and the reassessment period on an unfiled or incomplete T1135
extends beyond the normal window — which is why a form nobody owes money on is the item worth getting
right first.

| What T1135 asks for | What it does not ask for |
|---|---|
| Cost amount of the property in Canadian dollars | Its market value |
| The country the property sits in | An appraisal |
| Income earned from it during the year | Greek tax paid, which belongs on other schedules |
| The maximum cost amount held during the year | Anything about the Golden Visa itself |

## Cost amount, not market value, and why the deed-date rate is permanent

T1135 reports **cost amount**: for a Greek purchase, the price plus capitalised acquisition costs,
converted to Canadian dollars. The conversion happens at the rate applying on the deed date, and that
figure then sits in your file for as long as you own the property.

That has a consequence most buyers do not consider until it is fixed. A €400,000 purchase does not have
one Canadian cost — it has the Canadian cost that the CAD/EUR rate produced on one specific day, and in
practice not even that, because the price is rarely paid in a single movement. A typical file pays a
reservation deposit, then a tenth of the price on the preliminary agreement, then the balance at the
notarial deed some three to six months later, and each of those crosses the currency at whatever rate
applied that morning.

The Canadian cost base is therefore assembled from three or four different rates
and is fixed forever at those numbers. Two Canadians buying identical €400,000 apartments six months
apart can end up with cost bases differing by tens of thousands of dollars, and neither of them can
adjust it afterwards, because the cost amount reported on Form T1135 and the base used for the eventual
capital gain both run on the historical rate rather than on today's. Record the date, the rate and the
Canadian dollar amount for every payment as it leaves, and keep the bank advice: reconstructing this from
Greek notarial records years later is expensive and sometimes impossible.

Nothing about this is avoidable, and it is not a reason to time a purchase around currency. It is a
reason to record the rate, the date and the components of the cost properly at the transfer, because
reconstructing them years later from Greek notarial documents is expensive.

## Quebec residents file the same thing twice

A Quebec resident holding more than CAD 100,000 of foreign property files the federal T1135 **and**
Revenu Québec form TP-1079.8.BE. Quebec is the only province in this series with a separate provincial
foreign-property disclosure, and the two forms are not interchangeable.

This is the sort of item an accountant outside Quebec will not raise unprompted. Ask directly whether
your provincial filing position adds anything, and get the answer before the first return rather than
after a reassessment.

## Residence, not citizenship, and the door it leaves open

Because Canada taxes on residence, a Canadian who moves to Greece and genuinely severs Canadian
residential ties stops filing Canadian returns on worldwide income — an option no American in this series
has.

Two things sit in the way of treating that as simple. Ceasing Canadian residence triggers a deemed
disposition of most property at fair market value on the departure date, so the exit has a tax cost of
its own. And severing residence is a facts-and-circumstances test rather than an election: a house kept
in Canada, a spouse remaining, provincial health coverage and ongoing memberships all count against a
claim to have left.

Anyone considering that route should also read Greece's own side of it, because the
[Article 5A non-dom regime](/guides/greece-non-dom-tax-regime-5a-guide/) and the
[7% pensioner regime](/guides/greece-7-percent-pension-tax-retirees/) are what make Greek residence
attractive in the first place.

## Death is taxed in both countries, on two different things

Canada has no inheritance tax. Greece does. A Canadian owner and their heirs are therefore exposed to
both systems at once, on bases that do not correspond, and the relief a Canadian would expect from the
treaty does not arrive.

What Canada charges on death is a deemed disposition. The deceased is treated as having sold their
property at fair market value immediately before death, and the capital gain accrued over the whole
holding period becomes taxable on the final return. The tax is on the gain, it falls on the estate, and
the beneficiaries take the property at its stepped-up value.

What Greece charges is inheritance tax. That falls on each heir, it is computed on the value of that
heir's share rather than on any gain, and the rate depends on how closely related the heir was to the
deceased rather than on how the property performed.

Those are two different taxes, on two different taxpayers, measured on two different amounts. Foreign tax
credit relief generally works by crediting foreign tax against domestic tax on the same income in the
hands of the same person, and none of those three conditions is satisfied here. The realistic planning
assumption is that both charges apply and neither relieves the other. Take advice on the specific facts
rather than the general position, because the amounts involved make it worth doing properly.

Probate is the administrative half of the same problem. A Greek property generally needs a Greek
notarial acceptance regardless of what the Canadian estate does, so the estate runs two processes on two
timetables.

Two consequences worth acting on while the owner is alive. The Canadian charge is on the gain, so it is
sensitive to the cost base — which is denominated in Canadian dollars and assembled from the exchange
rates on the days each payment was made, as this page has already set out. Keeping those records is
estate planning, not just tax compliance. And the Greek charge is sensitive to who inherits, so the
question of which heirs take the Greek property, and in what shares, has a price attached that the
Canadian side of the estate does not have.

Currency is the quiet variable in all of it. The Canadian charge is computed in Canadian dollars while
the asset, the rent and the eventual price are in euros, so a move in the exchange rate alone can create a
Canadian taxable gain on a property that has not risen in euro terms.

There is a registered-plan point that follows from the same structure. Foreign real property cannot be
held inside an RRSP, a TFSA or a RRIF, so a Canadian buying in Greece is doing so with after-tax money
outside every shelter the domestic system offers. That is not a defect of the Greek purchase; it is a
comparison worth making explicitly against a domestic alternative that could sit inside a plan.

## Advantages and disadvantages for a Canadian buyer

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Residence-based taxation leaves a genuine exit route | Departure tax applies to the exit, on a deemed disposition |
| A double tax treaty with Greece gives credit for Greek tax paid | T1135 obligations begin at purchase, before any income exists |
| No filing obligation at all once residence is properly severed | Quebec residents carry a duplicate provincial disclosure |
| Greek rates on rental income are often absorbed by the credit | The CAD/EUR rate on deed date permanently fixes the cost base |

## Risks a Canadian buyer should price before signing

- **Treating T1135 as optional** because no Canadian tax is owed. The penalty regime is for the missing
  form, not for missing tax.
- **Reconstructing the cost amount later.** Capture the deed price, the acquisition costs and the
  exchange rate at the transfer.
- **Assuming an accountant knows the Quebec form applies.** Ask.
- **Underestimating what severing residence requires**, and the departure tax it triggers.
- **Holding through a corporation without advice**, because a foreign corporation holding foreign
  property brings its own Canadian information-return regime.

## Buyer scenarios where the Canadian position matters

**The Toronto or Vancouver buyer who stays Canadian.** Nothing changes about your residency. Budget the
T1135 filing annually from year one, plus a Quebec form if applicable, and treat the Greek permit purely
as travel freedom.

**The buyer planning to retire to Greece.** The interesting question is the order of operations: the
departure-tax consequences of ceasing Canadian residence, set against the Greek regimes available on
arrival. That sequence is worth modelling with advisers on both sides before the purchase, not after.

**The buyer holding through a company.** Ask what the structure does under Canadian foreign-affiliate
and information-return rules before it is created. A wrapper chosen for Greek convenience can be
expensive in Canada.

## The filing calendar, and what each form actually asks

A Canadian owner of Greek property is running two calendars: the Greek one, which produces the tax, and
the Canadian one, which produces the disclosure. They do not align, and the second is the one with
penalties attached to the form rather than to the tax.

| Filing | Threshold | Basis | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greek income tax return | Any Greek rental income | Gross rent less a flat 5% repairs allowance | Greek tax year, filed in Greece |
| Federal Form T1135 | CAD 100,000 cost amount | Cost amount in Canadian dollars | With the T1 return |
| Revenu Québec TP-1079.8.BE | CAD 100,000, Quebec residents | Same cost basis as T1135 | With the Quebec return |

The Greek charge is applied to gross rent after a flat 5% repairs allowance and nothing else, on the
progressive scale the [rental income guide](/guides/greece-rental-income-tax-non-resident/) sets out in
full. A €400,000 Kalamata or Crete apartment letting at €1,200 a month produces €14,400 of gross
rent, which the allowance reduces to €13,680, and that lands across the first two bands for roughly
€2,220 of Greek tax before ENFIA, management or vacancy are counted at all.

The Canadian return then
computes the same property on net rental profit after actual expenses, so the two countries are taxing
different numbers from the same tenancy, and the Greek tax is creditable against Canadian tax on that
income under the treaty rather than being an additional cost. The divergence is structural rather than
occasional. Greece allows 5% of gross and nothing else; Canada allows the management fee, the insurance,
the ENFIA, the interest and the repairs actually paid, and permits capital cost allowance on top. A €400,000
apartment with a €14,400 rent roll and running costs near a third of it produces a Greek liability of
about €2,220 and a much smaller Canadian profit, or none. Where the Canadian computation produces a
loss and the Greek one produces a bill — which happens whenever real expenses exceed the 5% Greece is
willing to recognise — there is no Canadian tax on that income for the credit to attach to, and the
Greek charge is simply paid.

Two Canadian-specific traps sit in that arithmetic. The Canadian computation is on net rental profit
after actual expenses, so the two countries are taxing different numbers, and a property can show a
Canadian loss and a Greek bill in the same year. And the T1135 threshold is measured on cost, so it is
crossed at purchase regardless of whether the property ever earns anything.

## One more Canadian item: the principal residence exemption does not travel

A Canadian's principal residence exemption can shelter the gain on a home, and it is not limited by geography in the way most owners assume — a property outside Canada can in principle be designated, provided the ordinarily-inhabited test is met for the years claimed. The difficulty is factual rather than legal: a Greek apartment let on the twelve-month terms a Golden Visa requires is, by construction, not ordinarily inhabited by the owner in those years.

That produces a choice most buyers never articulate. Let the property and it earns income and supports the permit, but the years it is let are years it cannot be designated. Keep it for personal use and the designation argument improves while the income disappears. Only one property per family unit can be designated for any given year in any case, so the calculation runs against whatever else you own. Put this to a Canadian adviser before the first tenancy rather than at the sale.
